*Prepared for a Primaries & Co-investments (LP / fund-investing) internship. Designed to be studied offline. Every non-obvious claim is numbered to a source in [Part 12](#part-12--sources-and-where-they-disagree). Where authoritative sources genuinely disagree, that is flagged explicitly.*

---

## Read this first: kill the "free alpha" myth before your interview

The sell-side framing you will hear is that co-investments are "free alpha": no fee, no carry, access to the GP's best deals. Lead with the counterargument, because that is what separates a thoughtful LP analyst from someone reciting a pitch deck.

The single most cited academic paper on the subject, Fang, Ivashina and Lerner (2015), studied 20 years of direct deals by seven large institutions and found that co-investments **underperformed the very funds they were made alongside**, consistent with **adverse selection**: GPs may share the deals they least want to hold alone, and co-investors lack the information edge to tell the difference. [7] Solo direct deals, by contrast, outperformed. [7]

A later paper using a larger, cleaner sample, Braun, Jenkinson and Schemmerl (2020), found **no evidence of adverse selection**: gross returns on co-invested deals looked like the other deals in the same funds, and once you strip out fees and carry, a **diversified portfolio** of co-investments beats the fund net of fees. [8]

So the honest position is not "free alpha" and not "trap." It is this: **co-investing is a fee-saving machine that hands you a return cushion of roughly 12% to 24% of gross MOIC (quantified in [Example 4](#example-4--the-fee-cushion-and-the-adverse-selection-break-even)). Whether you keep that cushion depends entirely on selection, market timing, and diversification, none of which are free.** Everything else in this guide is downstream of that sentence.

## Part 1 — What a co-investment is

A **co-investment** is a direct equity investment by an LP into a single portfolio company, made **alongside** a GP's fund but **outside** the fund's main commingled structure. [1][2] The GP leads the deal: it sources, negotiates, structures, controls, and monitors the company. The co-investing LP rides along in a minority, passive, non-controlling position. [1]

The defining mechanics:

- **It is deal-by-deal, not blind-pool.** When you commit to a primary fund, you are backing a manager to find deals you cannot yet see. A co-investment is a specific, named company you can underwrite today.
- **It is usually fee-light or fee-free.** Because the capital sits outside the fund, the co-investor typically pays little or no management fee and little or no carried interest on it. [1][3] This is the economic heart of the product and is detailed in [Part 4](#part-4--the-economics-no-feeno-carry-and-the-blended-return-impact).
- **It is almost always structured through a special purpose vehicle (SPV).** The GP sets up a single-asset SPV (often an LP or LLC) into which co-investors commit. Capital is typically **called and deployed almost immediately**, not over a multi-year drawdown like a fund. [2][5]
- **The co-investor is usually an existing LP in the GP's fund**, though GPs sometimes invite prospective LPs or third parties. [1][2]

Think of a primary fund commitment as buying a diversified, professionally managed basket on a delayed-deployment schedule, and a co-investment as cherry-picking one holding out of that basket, at a steep discount on fees, but bearing that single holding's idiosyncratic risk in full.

## Part 2 — Why GPs offer co-investments

GPs do not give away economics out of generosity. They offer co-investment because it solves concrete problems:

1. **Check-size / capacity on large deals.** A single fund will not (and per its own concentration limits often *cannot*) write the full equity check for a large target. Rather than club with a rival sponsor or take on more leverage, the GP fills the equity gap with LP co-investment capital. This is the original and still dominant driver. [4]
2. **Diversification and concentration discipline.** Most LPAs cap how much of a single fund can go into one deal (commonly 10% to 20% of commitments). Co-investment lets the GP do a big deal without breaching its own concentration limit.
3. **Fundraising leverage and LP relationship management.** Offering co-investment, especially no-fee/no-carry, is a powerful sweetener used to reward the largest and most strategically important LPs and to win their commitments to the *next* fund. [1] Co-investment access has become an explicit term LPs negotiate during primary fundraising.
4. **Speed and certainty of capital.** In a competitive auction, a sponsor that can show committed equity moves faster and bids more credibly. Reliable co-investors are a competitive weapon.
5. **It is cheaper than the alternatives.** The GP avoids bringing in another control sponsor (and sharing governance) or over-equitizing the fund.

The uncomfortable corollary, which you must hold in your head: **a GP that needs your capital on a deal has, by definition, decided not to put more of its own fund's capital in.** That is sometimes benign (concentration limits, fund nearly fully invested, deal larger than the fund's sweet spot) and sometimes a warning sign (the GP's own conviction is limited). Telling those apart is the job. See [Part 5](#part-5--adverse-selection-the-central-debate).

## Part 3 — Why LPs want co-investments

1. **Net-return enhancement through fee savings.** Co-investments paying no fee and no carry preserve the gross return that fees and carry would otherwise skim. On standard "2 and 20" economics this is worth a large fraction of the gross multiple (quantified in [Examples 1, 2 and 4](#part-16--worked-numerical-examples)). LPs explicitly pursue co-investment as fee-efficient access to private equity. [10][12]
2. **Lower blended cost of the whole program.** A primary commitment plus a no-fee co-invest sleeve produces a lower all-in fee load and a higher blended net return than the fund alone. See [Example 3](#example-3--blended-portfolio-return).
3. **Capital deployment / "putting money to work."** Large allocators struggle to deploy at scale; co-investment lets them deploy more dollars with a manager they already trust, faster than fund commitments alone permit. [3]
4. **Selectivity and control over exposure.** Co-investing is a *lever*: an LP can tilt toward sectors, geographies, or deal types it wants more of, and away from those it does not. [5]
5. **Transparency and information.** Unlike a blind-pool commitment, the LP sees the actual company, model, and thesis before committing. [2]
6. **J-curve mitigation.** Immediate deployment with no fee drag means capital starts working sooner and is not eroded by years of management fees, flattening and shortening the J-curve. See [Part 8](#part-8--single-asset-concentration-risk-and-j-curve-mitigation).
7. **Relationship deepening and capability building.** Co-investing strengthens the GP relationship and builds the LP's own direct-investing muscle, a stepping stone toward direct/solo investing for the most sophisticated allocators.

Demand structurally exceeds supply. A StepStone survey of 145 GPs and 420 funds cited in Bain's 2025 Global Private Equity Report found co-investment volume up roughly 30% versus pre-pandemic, and that **only about half of the LPs with appetite for co-investment were actually able to participate.** [4] Co-investment capacity is rationed, which is precisely why GPs can use it as a relationship currency.

## Part 4 — The economics: no-fee/no-carry and the blended-return impact

### The fund baseline: "2 and 20"

A standard buyout fund charges roughly a **2% annual management fee** and **20% carried interest** on profits, the latter payable only after LPs receive their capital back plus a **preferred return (hurdle)**, commonly **8%**, often followed by a **GP catch-up**. [11] Management fees frequently **step down** after the investment period (e.g., from committed capital to invested capital). [11]

These two charges attack returns from different angles:

- **Management fee** is a drag on *capital*: the LP pays it whether or not the fund makes money, and it reduces the capital actually put to work.
- **Carried interest** is a drag on *profit*: it skims 20% (above the hurdle) of the upside.

### The co-investment difference

Co-investments are typically **no-fee/no-carry**, or at least materially fee-reduced. [1][3] The GP is generally **not directly compensated on the co-invested portion** of the deal. [9] That means:

> **Net co-investment return ≈ gross co-investment return.**

That single identity is the whole product. On a deal where the fund LP nets 2.2x after carry, the co-investor on the same deal at the same entry keeps the full 2.5x. The 0.3x difference is pure fee saving, not skill. (Worked in [Example 1](#example-1--carried-interest-saved-on-a-single-deal).)

### Why this matters for blended program returns

LPs do not co-invest instead of committing to funds; they do both. The right mental model is a **blended portfolio**: most capital in the fund at net-of-fee returns, a sleeve in co-investments at gross returns. The co-invest sleeve drags the blended fee load down and the blended net return up. (Worked in [Example 3](#example-3--blended-portfolio-return).)

### The cushion this buys

The fee/carry saving is equivalent to a **return cushion**: a co-investment can underperform the fund's deals on a *gross* basis and still match or beat the fund on a *net* basis, because the co-investor is not paying the toll. Quantifying that cushion (roughly 12% of gross MOIC from carry alone, up to ~24% once management fees are included) is the cleanest way to reason about adverse selection. (Worked in [Example 4](#example-4--the-fee-cushion-and-the-adverse-selection-break-even).)

### Caveats you should not skip

- "No fee/no carry" is the norm, not a law. Some GPs charge reduced fees or carry on co-invest, or impose them on dedicated co-investment *funds* (as opposed to deal-by-deal SPVs). Read the SPV terms.
- Co-invest is not cost-free to the LP. The LP bears its own underwriting, legal, and ongoing monitoring costs, plus a share of SPV/deal expenses. These are real and rise with deal complexity.

## Part 5 — Adverse selection: the central debate

This is the most important conceptual topic in co-investment, and it is genuinely contested in the literature. Master both sides.

### The theory

**Adverse selection** (the "lemons" problem) says: if the GP knows more about a deal than you do, and gets to choose which deals to share, then the deals offered to you may be systematically worse than the deals the GP keeps for the fund alone. The GP might share deals that are too large for conviction, too risky, or where it wants to lay off exposure. You, the co-investor, cannot easily distinguish a "shared because of capacity" deal from a "shared because we are nervous" deal. This is sometimes called a **winner's curse** for co-investors.

### The bear case: Fang, Ivashina and Lerner (2015)

Studied direct investments by **seven large institutions over 1991 to 2011**. Findings: [7]

- Direct investments beat **public-market** indices, especially buyouts and 1990s vintages.
- Outperformance versus the **private-equity fund** benchmarks was limited and concentrated in buyouts.
- **Co-investments underperformed the corresponding funds**, consistent with **apparent adverse selection** in the deals made available.
- **Solo** (no-GP-alongside) deals **outperformed**, suggesting an information-resolution advantage that co-investors lack.
- Co-investments were also **poorly timed**, concentrated in large deals near market peaks.

### The bull case: Braun, Jenkinson and Schemmerl (2020)

Used a **large deal-level sample from three fund-of-funds managers**, covering the complete fund and deal history of **464 funds** regardless of whether the FoF actually invested, so it is free of the FoF's own selection bias. Findings: [8]

- **No evidence of adverse selection.** Gross-return (PME) distributions of co-investable deals and other deals in the same funds were statistically similar.
- Co-investments carry **lower costs** to investors.
- Simulating net returns under a range of plausible fee structures, **buyout co-investment net returns were economically and statistically higher than fund net returns**, and **reasonably sized diversified portfolios** of co-investments **significantly outperform** the funds.
- The critical caveat: this is a **portfolio** result. One-off deals carry high dispersion; the outperformance comes from diversification plus the fee saving, not from individual deal magic.

### Practitioner data points

- **Cambridge Associates ("Making Waves", 2015)** compared 104 co-investments to the 64 funds that sponsored them and asked "Is adverse selection real?" Co-investments outperformed most in **low-volume vintage years** (early 2000s, 2009), i.e., when markets were volatile, competition was thin, and entry multiples were low. The implication: *when* you co-invest matters as much as *whether*. [6] **Flag:** that study compares co-investments **gross of fees** against sponsoring funds **net of fees**, which structurally tilts the result in favor of co-investments. Treat its magnitude with caution. [6]
- **StepStone data (via Chronograph)** found **pre-signing co-investments averaged 2.7x gross TVPI versus 2.2x for post-signing**, suggesting earlier-stage, less-competitive access performs better. [10]
- **Adams Street (2025)** argues from practice that adverse selection is a "myth," reasoning that GPs depend on long-term LP relationships and would damage them by dumping bad deals, and that disciplined co-investors who select rather than "do every deal" can outperform. [9] Note this is an interested party (a co-investment manager).

### How to reconcile it (your synthesis for the interview)

The papers reach opposite headline conclusions partly because they measure different things:

- FIL measures the **realized choices of specific institutions**, which bundles together what deals they were *offered*, what they *picked*, and *when*. BJS measures the **full deal universe** of the funds, stripping out investor selection.
- FIL finds co-invests gross-*underperform* the fund's deals; BJS finds gross *parity*. Both agree the **fee saving helps net returns**.
- Where they converge: **timing and concentration dominate**. Co-investment supply peaks in frothy markets (large deals, high multiples), which is exactly when forward returns are worst. A program that mechanically accepts everything will load up at the top of the cycle and double down on existing exposures. Cambridge explicitly **advises against a "do every deal" approach** for this reason. [5]

**The defensible LP position:** adverse selection is a *real risk to be managed*, not a reason to avoid co-investing. You manage it with deal-by-deal selectivity, checking the deal's fit to the GP's stated **strike zone**, scrutinizing GP alignment and motive for sharing, avoiding over-deployment in hot markets, and **diversifying** across enough deals that idiosyncratic and selection risk wash out. [5][8]

## Part 6 — Syndicated co-invest vs co-underwriting

These are the two structural flavors of co-investment, and the distinction is a favorite interview question.

### Syndicated (post-signing) co-investment

- The GP has **already won and signed** the deal (or is certain to). It then **syndicates** the excess equity to co-investors after the fact.
- The deal is de-risked from an execution standpoint: the company is bought, terms are set.
- Diligence is more of a **review** exercise: the co-investor evaluates a completed deal and the GP's diligence rather than running its own from scratch.
- Lower execution risk, but typically **more competitive** access and, on the data, **lower returns** (StepStone: ~2.2x gross TVPI post-signing). [10]

### Co-underwriting (pre-signing) co-investment

- The GP invites the LP in **while still competing for the asset**, sometimes before exclusivity is even secured. [5]
- The GP **conducts diligence in real time and shares it with LPs as it goes.** [5]
- There is **no certainty the GP will win**: the LP can spend weeks of work for a deal that never happens. [5]
- It demands far more from the LP: faster commitment, deeper independent analysis, and the infrastructure to act as a genuine co-underwriter. **GPs increasingly prefer LPs who can co-underwrite**, and reward them with better access. [10]
- Less competition for the asset and, on the data, **higher returns** (StepStone: ~2.7x gross TVPI pre-signing). [10]

### The trade-off

Co-underwriting is harder, riskier on execution (deal may die), and operationally demanding, but it is where the better-access, better-return deals tend to sit. Syndicated co-invest is easier and lower-risk on execution, but more crowded. The strategic shift in the market is toward LPs building the capability to co-underwrite. [10]

## Part 7 — Deal timelines and the speed demand on the LP

Speed is the operational signature of co-investment, and it is the single biggest difference from primary fund work.

| | Primary fund DD | Co-investment DD |
|---|---|---|
| Typical timeline | 4 to 12 weeks, with flexibility [from LP-side fund DD norms] | 2 to 4 weeks, sometimes days, on the GP's clock [5] |
| What is being judged | A manager and a blind pool | A specific, named asset |
| Who sets the clock | Largely the LP | The GP's deal process and the seller's timeline |
| Certainty | The fund will close | The deal may never happen (co-underwriting) [5] |

Key points:

- **Co-underwriting can require a commitment in three to four weeks, with no certainty the GP even wins the deal.** [5]
- The LP is on the **GP's** timeline, which is on the **seller's** timeline. There is no extension to suit the LP's IC calendar.
- This forces process discipline: pre-cleared diligence frameworks, a standing IC that can convene fast, template legal docs, and a clear "fast no." An LP that cannot decide quickly will simply stop being offered deals.
- **Speed is itself a selection pressure.** The best deals go to the LPs who can move; slow LPs get adversely selected into the leftovers. This is a practical channel through which adverse selection becomes self-inflicted.

## Part 8 — Single-asset concentration risk and J-curve mitigation

### Concentration risk

A primary fund holds many companies (commonly 10 to 25 in a buyout fund), so any one failure is diluted. A co-investment is **one company**. If it impairs, the co-investor takes the full loss on that capital, undiluted. [2]

The mathematics is unforgiving. With an equal-weighted, roughly independent set of deals, portfolio return dispersion falls with the square root of the number of deals:

> **σ_portfolio ≈ σ_deal / √N**

A single co-investment bears the full σ_deal. A program of 20 co-investments cuts dispersion to about **22%** (1 / √20) of a single deal's. (Worked in [Example 5](#example-5--concentration-and-the-power-of-diversification).) This is precisely why Braun, Jenkinson and Schemmerl frame their outperformance result as a **diversified-portfolio** result and warn against one-offs. [8]

**Practical implication:** a co-investment program needs enough deals to diversify (the literature points to "reasonably sized portfolios"), conscious sector/vintage/manager spread, and per-deal sizing limits so no single impairment is fatal. Doing "every deal" a GP offers is the opposite of risk management; it concentrates you into whatever the market is overproducing.

### J-curve mitigation

The **J-curve** is the early-life dip in a fund's net return / NAV. It happens because, in the first few years, the fund **draws and pays management fees** and **deploys capital into companies that have not yet appreciated or exited**, so reported net performance starts negative and only later curves up as value is realized. The shape on a chart resembles a "J."

Co-investments flatten and shorten the J-curve through two mechanisms:

1. **No fee drag.** With no annual management fee, there is no fee-driven erosion pulling early NAV below cost.
2. **Immediate deployment.** SPV capital is called and put to work essentially at once, rather than sitting as an uncalled commitment or being deployed slowly over a multi-year investment period. [2][5] Capital that is working is capital that can appreciate.

Cambridge's analysis shows co-investment vehicles with shallower, shorter J-curves than their sponsoring funds. [6]

**Illustrative J-curve (net TVPI by year, hypothetical):**

| Year | Fund (2 and 20) | No-fee co-invest |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.92x | 1.00x |
| 2 | 0.88x | 1.05x |
| 3 | 0.95x | 1.20x |
| 4 | 1.15x | 1.45x |
| 5 | 1.45x | 1.80x |

The fund dips below 1.00x for the first few years (fees plus undeployed/unrealized capital); the co-invest starts at cost and climbs. These figures are illustrative, not measured, but the *shape* is the point.

**Caveat:** mitigating the J-curve is not the same as reducing risk. Faster deployment means faster exposure. You shorten the time to positive marks by accepting concentrated single-asset risk immediately.

## Part 9 — Governance and information rights

Co-investors are **passive, minority, non-controlling**. The GP runs the company; the co-investor does not get a board seat or operational say in the typical structure. [1] This has two consequences:

1. **You are underwriting the GP's stewardship as much as the asset.** You depend on the GP to govern, add value, and exit well. If you have low conviction in the GP, do not co-invest.
2. **Your protections are contractual, set in the SPV/co-invest documents, not granted by control.** These are the terms to read and negotiate:

- **Information rights:** frequency and depth of financial and portfolio reporting, access to the company's reporting, valuation methodology, and timing.
- **Economics in writing:** confirm no-fee/no-carry (or the exact reduced terms), expense allocation between the SPV and co-investors, and treatment of transaction/monitoring fees.
- **Alignment and fee offsets:** does the GP run the co-invest pari passu with the fund (same entry price, same instrument, same terms)? Any difference is a red flag.
- **Liquidity and exit:** **drag-along** (the GP can force you to sell alongside the fund) and **tag-along** (you can sell alongside the GP) rights, transfer restrictions, and what happens if you want out early (usually little; co-invest is illiquid).
- **Follow-on capital:** are you obligated or merely permitted to fund follow-ons / rescue rounds, and on what terms? Unfunded follow-on obligations are a hidden risk.
- **Conflicts:** how are conflicts handled if the company interacts with other GP portfolio companies or funds? What happens in a restructuring or continuation-vehicle scenario?
- **Capital-call and default mechanics** in the SPV.

The governing reference for what "good" looks like is ILPA's guidance, which stresses alignment, transparency (GPs should disclose co-invest arrangements to all LPs), and that suitable opportunities should be allocated to the fund first. [3]

## Part 10 — How co-investment underwriting differs from primary fund DD

This contrast is the conceptual core of a Primaries & Co-investments role. Know it cold.

| Dimension | Primary fund DD | Co-investment DD |
|---|---|---|
| Object of analysis | A **manager** and a strategy (blind pool) | A **single, named asset** |
| Direction | **Top-down / qualitative**: team, track record, strategy, process, alignment, terms | **Bottom-up / quantitative**: this company's market, model, leverage, valuation, downside |
| Track record | Fund-level IRR/TVPI/DPI, persistence, attribution across many deals | This deal's thesis; GP's **co-investment** track record specifically [5] |
| Key risk | Manager selection; will the GP deliver over a decade | Deal selection and **adverse selection**; single-company impairment |
| Diversification | Built in (many deals, multiple vintages) | **None** at the single-deal level; must be built across the program |
| Time horizon | Decision can take weeks to months | **Days to weeks**, on the GP's clock [5] |
| Reliance on GP | Judging the GP's general capability | Heavily **relying on and re-checking the GP's specific diligence**, especially co-underwriting where DD is shared live [5] |
| Output | Commit / pass on a 10-to-14-year relationship | Buy / pass on one company, now |
| Core question | "Is this a good manager?" | "Is this a good deal, *and* why are they sharing it with me?" |

The non-negotiable extra question in co-investment is **"Why is this deal being offered to me at all?"** That question does not exist in primary fund work, and forgetting to ask it is how LPs get adversely selected.

## Part 11 — Underwriting a single co-investment deal

Underwrite across six lenses. The first five are standard deal diligence; the sixth (downside) is where LP co-investors must be most disciplined, because they cannot rely on a diversified pool to bail out a bad call.

### 1. Market
- Size, growth, and structure (TAM/SAM, growth rate, cyclicality, fragmentation).
- Competitive dynamics and the company's defensible position (moat, switching costs, share, pricing power).
- Secular tailwinds vs headwinds (regulation, technology disruption, demand durability).
- Where are we in the cycle? Co-investment supply peaks in frothy markets; ask whether you are buying at a multiple high. [5][6]

### 2. Company / business quality
- Business model and unit economics; how does it actually make money and is that durable?
- Revenue quality: recurring vs one-time, customer concentration, churn, contract length, backlog.
- Margins and operating leverage; cash conversion (does EBITDA become cash?).
- Competitive position and the specific levers for value creation (organic growth, M&A roll-up, margin expansion, multiple re-rating).

### 3. Management
- Quality, tenure, incentives, and equity stake (alignment).
- Track record executing the kind of plan being underwritten.
- The GP's relationship with and view of management; any planned changes.

### 4. Financials and the model
- Historical financials: growth, margin trajectory, working-capital and capex intensity, one-offs/normalizations to EBITDA (scrutinize add-backs).
- The **base-case model** and whether its assumptions are defensible or heroic.
- **Leverage:** entry debt/EBITDA, structure (fixed vs floating), maturities, covenants, interest coverage, headroom. Higher leverage magnifies both equity returns and the probability of impairment.
- **Returns bridge / value-creation bridge:** decompose the projected equity return into **EBITDA growth**, **margin expansion**, **multiple change**, and **debt paydown / deleveraging**. A thesis that leans mostly on **multiple expansion** is fragile; one driven by EBITDA growth and deleveraging is sturdier.

### 5. Deal structure
- **Entry valuation:** EV/EBITDA (and revenue where relevant) vs comparable transactions and trading comps. Is the price disciplined or full?
- **Capital structure and the LP's instrument:** is the co-investor pari passu with the fund (same security, same price, same terms)? Any structural subordination is a red flag.
- **Sources and uses; pro-forma ownership; the co-invest's slice.**
- **Fees/economics on the co-invest** (confirm no-fee/no-carry or the exact terms), expense allocation, follow-on obligations.
- **Alignment:** GP's own dollars in the deal (fund commitment, GP commitment), and management's rollover equity.

### 6. Downside / risk
- **Downside case and break-even:** what set of assumptions returns 1.0x? How plausible is it?
- **Loss scenario:** what does a recession / demand shock / margin compression / refinancing failure do to the equity? Stress leverage explicitly.
- **Liquidity and exit:** realistic exit routes (strategic, sponsor-to-sponsor, IPO), hold period, and exit-multiple assumptions. Sensitize the exit multiple, because it is usually the swing variable.
- **Concentration fit:** how does this deal change the LP's overall exposure (sector, geography, vintage, single-name)? Does it double down on something you already own through the fund? [5]
- **The meta-question:** why is the GP sharing this, and does the answer survive scrutiny?

## Part 12 The co-investment IC memo

### Purpose and structure

The IC memo is the document on which the investment committee approves or rejects the co-investment. Under co-investment time pressure it must be **decision-ready, honest about risk, and explicit about why the deal is being shared.** A good co-invest memo differs from a fund memo by being **bottom-up and deal-specific**, and by always containing an explicit **adverse-selection / "why us" section**.

### Sample co-investment IC memo outline

```
1.  EXECUTIVE SUMMARY / RECOMMENDATION
    - Recommendation (invest / pass) and proposed amount
    - Company, sector, geography, lead GP, fund alongside
    - Headline thesis in 3 to 5 bullets
    - Key terms: entry EV / EBITDA, leverage, ownership, fees (no-fee/no-carry?)
    - Base / upside / downside return (gross and net MOIC and IRR)
    - Top 3 risks and the single biggest reason this could lose money

2.  TRANSACTION OVERVIEW
    - Deal structure, sources & uses, pro-forma cap table
    - Co-investment size, the LP's slice, syndicate composition
    - Syndicated (post-signing) or co-underwritten (pre-signing)? Timeline & certainty
    - SPV terms summary: fees, expenses, follow-on, drag/tag, info rights

3.  WHY THIS DEAL IS BEING OFFERED TO US  [the section a fund memo lacks]
    - GP's stated reason (capacity, concentration limit, deal size, relationship)
    - GP's own capital in the deal (fund + GP commitment); management rollover
    - Fit to the GP's stated strategy / strike zone
    - Adverse-selection assessment: is the "why" benign or a warning?

4.  GP / SPONSOR ASSESSMENT
    - Relevance of the GP's experience to this asset and sector
    - GP's co-investment track record specifically
    - Alignment and any conflicts

5.  MARKET
6.  COMPANY / BUSINESS QUALITY
7.  MANAGEMENT
8.  FINANCIALS & MODEL
    - Historicals, base-case projections, key assumptions
    - Leverage and capital structure analysis
    - Value-creation bridge (EBITDA growth / margin / multiple / deleveraging)

9.  VALUATION
    - Entry multiple vs comps and precedent transactions
    - Returns analysis: base / upside / downside, gross and net
    - Sensitivity tables (exit multiple, EBITDA growth, leverage)

10. RISKS & MITIGANTS
    - Business, market, leverage/financing, execution, exit, concentration
    - Explicit downside / loss case and break-even

11. PORTFOLIO FIT
    - Effect on program exposures (sector / geography / vintage / single-name)
    - Diversification and sizing rationale

12. RECOMMENDATION & PROPOSED TERMS
    - Amount, conditions, approvals required, next steps & timeline

APPENDICES: full model, GP materials reviewed, third-party DD, legal summary
```

### What good looks like

- The **return analysis shows gross and net, base/upside/downside, with sensitivities**, not a single point estimate.
- The **"why us" section is real**, not a box-tick.
- The **downside case is specific and quantified**, with a stated break-even.
- The **value-creation bridge** is decomposed so the IC can see whether the thesis leans on fragile multiple expansion or on operational improvement.

## Part 13 — Key underwriting questions and red flags

### The questions to ask on every co-investment

1. **Why is this being offered to us?** Is the stated reason (capacity, concentration cap, deal size) consistent with the facts, or is the GP laying off risk?
2. **How much of the GP's own and the fund's capital is in this deal?** Misalignment is disqualifying.
3. **Are we pari passu with the fund?** Same instrument, same price, same terms?
4. **Does the deal fit the GP's stated strike zone**, or is it a stretch outside its proven competence? [5]
5. **What is the GP's co-investment track record specifically**, not just its fund track record? [5]
6. **What does the value-creation bridge depend on?** EBITDA/operations (good) or multiple expansion (fragile)?
7. **What does the downside / loss case look like, and what is break-even?**
8. **How much leverage, and does it survive a downturn and a refinancing?**
9. **How does this change our total portfolio exposure?** Are we doubling down? [5]
10. **What are the exit routes and how realistic is the exit-multiple assumption?**
11. **What are the fees, expenses, and follow-on obligations in the SPV?**
12. **Can we actually do the work in the time given?** If not, pass.

### Red flags

- The GP **cannot give a clean, consistent reason** for sharing the deal.
- **Thin or negative GP alignment**: little fund/GP capital, or the co-invest is worse-priced or structurally subordinate to the fund.
- **Co-invest economics dressed up as "free" but loaded with fees/expenses** in the SPV, or open-ended follow-on obligations.
- **Thesis driven mainly by multiple expansion** or by an aggressive exit multiple.
- **Aggressive EBITDA add-backs / normalizations** that inflate entry quality.
- **Stretched leverage** with tight covenants, floating-rate exposure, or near-term maturities.
- **Deal outside the GP's strike zone** (new sector, new geography, much larger than usual).
- **A "do every deal" posture** at the program level, especially heavy deployment in a hot market. [5][6]
- **Customer/supplier concentration**, regulatory overhang, or cyclicality that is glossed over.
- **Timeline so compressed** that genuine independent diligence is impossible. Speed pressure is a tactic as well as a constraint.
- The deal **doubles down on an exposure the LP already has** through the fund. [5]

## Part 14 — Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Co-investment** | A direct minority equity investment by an LP into a single company, made alongside a GP's fund but outside the main fund vehicle, usually fee-light or fee-free. [1] |
| **LP (Limited Partner)** | An investor that commits capital to a fund (pension, endowment, sovereign wealth fund, insurer, family office, fund-of-funds). |
| **GP (General Partner)** | The manager that raises and runs the fund, sources and controls deals, and earns fees and carry. |
| **Primary (commitment)** | A commitment of capital to a blind-pool fund, drawn down and invested by the GP over time. |
| **Blind pool** | A fund whose specific investments are unknown at commitment; the LP backs the manager, not named assets. |
| **SPV (Special Purpose Vehicle)** | A single-asset entity (LP/LLC) created to hold a co-investment; capital is typically called and deployed immediately. [2] |
| **Syndicated co-investment** | Co-investment offered after the GP has signed/closed the deal (post-signing); execution-de-risked but more competitive. [10] |
| **Co-underwriting** | Co-investment offered while the GP is still competing for the asset (pre-signing); DD shared in real time, fastest timelines, no certainty the GP wins. [5][10] |
| **Adverse selection** | The risk that the deals a GP shares are systematically worse than the deals it keeps, because the GP knows more and chooses what to offer. [7] |
| **Strike zone** | The GP's stated investment profile (size, sector, geography, deal type); a deal outside it is a red flag. [5] |
| **Management fee** | Annual fee (commonly ~2%) on committed or invested capital; a drag on capital regardless of performance. [11] |
| **Carried interest (carry)** | The GP's share of profits (commonly 20%) above the hurdle; a drag on upside. [11] |
| **"2 and 20"** | Shorthand for 2% management fee plus 20% carried interest. [11] |
| **Hurdle / preferred return** | The minimum LP return (commonly ~8%) before the GP earns carry. [11] |
| **GP catch-up** | After the hurdle, a tranche flowing mostly or wholly to the GP until it has its full carry share of profit. [11] |
| **Distribution waterfall** | The contractual order in which proceeds are split between LPs and GP (return of capital → pref → catch-up → carry split). [11] |
| **MOIC** | Multiple on Invested Capital = total value ÷ invested capital. |
| **TVPI** | Total Value to Paid-In = (distributions + residual NAV) ÷ paid-in capital = DPI + RVPI. |
| **DPI** | Distributions to Paid-In (realized cash returned ÷ paid-in). |
| **RVPI** | Residual Value to Paid-In (unrealized NAV ÷ paid-in). |
| **IRR** | Internal Rate of Return; the discount rate setting the NPV of cash flows to zero. |
| **J-curve** | The early-life dip in a fund's net return/NAV from fees and undeployed/unrealized capital, recovering later into a "J." |
| **PME (Public Market Equivalent)** | A method comparing private-equity cash flows to a public-index investment of the same timing; >1 means PE beat the index. The Kaplan-Schoar variant (KS-PME) is standard in academic work. [academic; see 19] |
| **Value-creation bridge** | Decomposition of equity return into EBITDA growth, margin expansion, multiple change, and debt paydown. |
| **Pari passu** | On equal footing; the co-investor holds the same instrument, price, and terms as the fund. |
| **Drag-along / tag-along** | Drag: the GP can compel the co-investor to sell alongside it; Tag: the co-investor can sell alongside the GP. |
| **Vintage year** | The year a fund/deal begins investing; a key dimension for diversification and benchmarking. |
| **Dry powder** | Committed but uninvested capital. |
| **DDQ** | Due Diligence Questionnaire. |
| **IC (Investment Committee)** | The body that approves or rejects an investment. |

## Part 15 — Key formulas

**Multiple on Invested Capital**
```
MOIC = Total Value (Distributions + Residual NAV) / Invested Capital
```

**Fund value multiples**
```
TVPI = (Distributions + Residual NAV) / Paid-in Capital = DPI + RVPI
DPI  = Distributions / Paid-in Capital
RVPI = Residual NAV  / Paid-in Capital
```

**No-fee/no-carry co-investment identity**
```
Net co-investment MOIC = Gross co-investment MOIC
```

**Preferred return (compounded over t years at hurdle h on capital C)**
```
Preferred Return = C * ((1 + h)^t - 1)
```

**GP catch-up amount (100% catch-up, carry rate c, on a preferred return P)**
```
X = (c / (1 - c)) * P
```
*(With c = 20%, X = 0.25 * P. After full catch-up, total GP carry equals exactly c x total profit above return of capital.)*

**Carry, hurdle cleared with full catch-up (shortcut)**
```
Carried Interest = c * (Total Distributions - Contributed Capital)
LP Net Profit    = (1 - c) * (Total Distributions - Contributed Capital)
```

**Blended program net MOIC (weights wᵢ summing to 1)**
```
Blended Net MOIC = Σ (wᵢ * Net MOICᵢ)
where wᵢ = capital in sleeve i / total capital
```

**Fee cushion / adverse-selection break-even**
```
Fee Cushion (in MOIC) = Fund Gross MOIC - Fund Net MOIC (on the same deal)
Break-even co-invest gross MOIC = Fund Net MOIC on the same deal
```
*(A co-investment can lag the fund's deals on gross return by up to the fee cushion and still match the fund net of fees.)*

**Portfolio dispersion (equal weights, independent deals)**
```
σ_portfolio ≈ σ_deal / √N
```

**IRR (definition)**
```
0 = Σ [ CFₜ / (1 + IRR)^t ]   for t = 0 ... T
```

**PME (concept; Kaplan-Schoar style)**
```
KS-PME = Σ (FV-adjusted Distributions) / Σ (FV-adjusted Contributions)
where cash flows are scaled by the return of a public index over the matching period;
KS-PME > 1 means PE outperformed the index.
```

## Part 16 — Worked numerical examples

*All figures are illustrative and built to be turned into calculators. Inputs are stated explicitly so each can be parameterized.*

### Example 1 — Carried interest saved on a single deal

**Inputs:** LP equity in the deal = **$10.0M**; gross exit multiple = **2.5x**; hurdle = **8%**; hold = **5 years**; carry = **20%**; **100% GP catch-up**.

**Step 1 — Gross outcome.**
Exit value = 2.5 × $10.0M = **$25.0M**. Gross profit = $25.0M − $10.0M = **$15.0M**.

**Step 2 — Through the fund (full waterfall).**
- Return of capital to LP: **$10.0M**.
- Preferred return: $10.0M × (1.08^5 − 1) = $10.0M × (1.46933 − 1) = **$4.693M**.
- GP catch-up: X = 0.20 / 0.80 × $4.693M = 0.25 × $4.693M = **$1.173M**.
- Remaining profit: $15.0M − $4.693M − $1.173M = **$9.134M**, split 80/20 → LP $7.307M, GP $1.827M.
- **GP carry total** = $1.173M + $1.827M = **$3.000M** (= 20% × $15.0M, the catch-up shortcut).
- **LP total** = $10.0M + $4.693M + $7.307M = **$22.0M → net MOIC 2.20x.**

**Step 3 — As a co-investment (no carry).**
LP keeps the full $25.0M → **MOIC 2.50x.**

**Result:** carry saved = **$3.0M**; MOIC uplift from not paying carry = **+0.30x** (2.50x vs 2.20x), about **13.6%** more profit, purely from fee economics, not skill.

---

### Example 2 — Fund-level gross-to-net wedge

**Inputs:** Commitment = **$100M**; lifetime management fees (illustrative) = **$15M** (e.g., 2%/yr on committed during a 5-yr investment period = $10M, then ~$5M on stepped-down invested capital thereafter); gross deal multiple = **2.5x**; carry = **20%**, hurdle cleared, full catch-up. Assume no recycling, so invested capital = $100M − $15M = **$85M**.

**Step 1 — Gross, no fees, no carry (reference).** Full $100M at 2.5x = **$250M → 2.50x.**

**Step 2 — Apply fund fees.** Only $85M is invested. Gross proceeds = 2.5 × $85M = **$212.5M**.

**Step 3 — Apply carry.** LP total contribution = $100M (funds both deals and fees). Net profit before carry = $212.5M − $100M = $112.5M. Carry = 20% × $112.5M = **$22.5M**. LP net = $212.5M − $22.5M = **$190.0M → net MOIC 1.90x.**

**Result:** gross **2.50x → net 1.90x**. The total wedge is **0.60x**, i.e., about **24%** of the gross multiple consumed by fees plus carry. A no-fee/no-carry co-investment on the same underlying deal would keep the full **2.50x**.

*(Note: the fee and carry effects are not cleanly additive, because fees reduce the capital that earns the multiple, which in turn shrinks the carry base. Use the two endpoints, 2.50x gross and 1.90x net, rather than asserting a clean split.)*

---

### Example 3 — Blended portfolio return

**Inputs:** Fund commitment = **$100M** at net **1.90x** (from Example 2); co-investment sleeve = **$25M** at net **2.50x** (no-fee/no-carry, same underlying deal quality).

**Step 1 — Total value by sleeve.**
- Fund: 100 × 1.90 = **$190M**.
- Co-invest: 25 × 2.50 = **$62.5M**.

**Step 2 — Blend.**
Total value = $190M + $62.5M = **$252.5M** on $125M deployed.
Blended net MOIC = $252.5M / $125M = **2.02x**.

**Result:** adding a 25% no-fee co-invest sleeve lifts the program's blended net MOIC from **1.90x to 2.02x** (**+0.12x**, about **+6%** relative), with the gain coming entirely from the fee structure of the sleeve.

---

### Example 4 — The fee cushion and the adverse-selection break-even

**Question:** how much can a co-investment underperform the fund's deals on a *gross* basis before the co-investor is worse off net?

**Inputs:** Fund's deals: gross **2.50x**. Fund LP net on the same deal after **carry only** = **2.20x** (Example 1). Fund LP net after **fees plus carry** = **1.90x** (Example 2). Co-investment: net = gross (no fees/carry).

**Carry-only cushion.** Break-even co-invest gross = fund net = **2.20x**. Cushion vs the fund's 2.50x gross = 2.50 − 2.20 = **0.30x = 12%** of gross MOIC.

**Fees-plus-carry cushion.** Break-even co-invest gross = fund net = **1.90x**. Cushion = 2.50 − 1.90 = **0.60x = 24%** of gross MOIC.

**Result:** the fee saving buys a cushion of roughly **12% to 24% of gross MOIC**. A co-investment can be that much worse on a gross basis and still match or beat the fund net of fees. This is the precise way to frame the [adverse-selection debate](#part-5--adverse-selection-the-central-debate): Fang-Ivashina-Lerner found co-invests gross-*underperform* [7]; Braun-Jenkinson-Schemmerl found gross *parity* [8]. Either way, the LP only loses net if the adverse selection exceeds this cushion. **The interview point:** adverse selection has to be quite severe to overwhelm the fee advantage, which is exactly why disciplined, diversified co-investing tends to win net even if individual deal selection is imperfect.

---

### Example 5 — Concentration and the power of diversification

**Inputs:** Per-deal return standard deviation σ_deal = **1.00x** of MOIC (illustrative); deals assumed roughly independent and equal-weighted.

**Step 1 — Single co-investment.** Dispersion borne = full **σ_deal = 1.00x**. If this one company impairs to zero, the loss is **100%** of the capital, undiluted.

**Step 2 — Diversified co-investment program of N = 20 deals.**
σ_portfolio ≈ σ_deal / √N = 1.00x / √20 = 1.00x / 4.472 = **0.224x**.

**Result:** moving from one deal to 20 cuts return dispersion to about **22%** of a single deal's, and caps any single impairment at roughly **5%** (1/20) of the program. A single total loss in a diversified fund of 15 to 25 names costs ~4% to 7% of the portfolio; the same total loss as a standalone co-invest costs **100%** of that capital. This is the mechanical reason Braun-Jenkinson-Schemmerl frame co-investment outperformance as a **diversified-portfolio** result and warn against one-offs. [8]

*(Calculator note: vary N and σ_deal; the √N relationship assumes independence and equal weights. Real deals are positively correlated, especially within a sector or vintage, which weakens the benefit, another argument for spreading across sectors and vintages.)*

## Part 17 — Flashcards

*Format: Question → model answer. Tagged [Sub-topic · Difficulty].*

**1. [Definitions · Easy]** What is a co-investment?
→ A direct minority equity investment by an LP into a single company, made alongside a GP's fund but outside the main fund vehicle, usually with little or no fee and carry. [1]

**2. [Definitions · Easy]** How is a co-investment usually structured?

→ Net-return enhancement: no-fee/no-carry economics preserve gross returns that fees and carry would otherwise skim. [10][12]

**7. [Rationale-LP · Medium]** Name four LP benefits beyond fee savings.
→ Faster capital deployment, selectivity over exposures, transparency into a named asset, and J-curve mitigation. (Also relationship deepening and capability building.) [2][3][5]

**8. [Economics · Easy]** What does "2 and 20" mean?
→ A 2% annual management fee plus 20% carried interest on profits above a hurdle. [11]

**9. [Economics · Medium]** What is the no-fee/no-carry identity?
→ Net co-investment return approximately equals gross co-investment return, because the capital sits outside the fee-bearing fund. [1]

**10. [Economics · Medium]** How does a management fee differ from carried interest in how it hurts returns?
→ Management fee is a drag on capital paid regardless of performance; carry is a drag on profit, taking ~20% of upside above the hurdle. [11]

**12. [Economics · Hard]** Roughly how much of a 2.5x gross multiple do 2-and-20 economics consume at the fund level?
→ About 24% (gross 2.50x to net ~1.90x), combining fees and carry under illustrative assumptions. (Example 2.)

**13. [Adverse Selection · Medium]** Define adverse selection in co-investment.
→ The risk that GPs share systematically worse deals than they keep, because they know more and choose what to offer; co-investors may face a winner's curse. [7]

**14. [Adverse Selection · Hard]** What did Fang, Ivashina and Lerner (2015) find?
→ Across seven institutions over 1991 to 2011, co-investments underperformed the funds they were made alongside (apparent adverse selection), while solo direct deals outperformed; co-invests were also poorly timed. [7]

**15. [Adverse Selection · Hard]** What did Braun, Jenkinson and Schemmerl (2020) find, and how does it differ?
→ Using a larger, selection-bias-free deal-level sample, they found no evidence of adverse selection: gross returns were similar, and diversified co-investment portfolios outperform funds net of fees. They contradict FIL on the headline. [8]

**16. [Adverse Selection · Hard]** How do you reconcile the two papers?
→ Different samples and methods (specific institutions' realized choices vs the full fund deal universe). Both agree fee savings help net returns. Convergence: timing and concentration dominate, and co-investment supply peaks in frothy markets. Adverse selection is a manageable risk, not a verdict. [5][6][7][8]

**17. [Adverse Selection · Medium]** What is the flaw in the Cambridge "Making Waves" adverse-selection comparison?
→ It compares co-investments gross of fees against sponsoring funds net of fees, which structurally favors co-investments and overstates the advantage. [6]

**18. [Adverse Selection · Medium]** How big does adverse selection have to be to make co-investing lose net?
→ Worse than the fee cushion, roughly 12% to 24% of gross MOIC under standard 2-and-20 assumptions. (Example 4.)

**19. [Syndicated vs Co-underwriting · Easy]** Define syndicated co-investment.
→ Co-investment offered after the GP has signed/closed the deal (post-signing); execution-de-risked but more competitive. [10]

**20. [Syndicated vs Co-underwriting · Medium]** Define co-underwriting.
→ Co-investment offered while the GP is still competing for the asset (pre-signing), with diligence shared in real time, fastest timelines, and no certainty the GP wins. [5][10]

**21. [Syndicated vs Co-underwriting · Medium]** Which performs better in the data, and why?
→ Pre-signing/co-underwritten deals (StepStone: ~2.7x gross TVPI) beat post-signing/syndicated (~2.2x), likely because they are less competed. [10]

**22. [Timelines · Easy]** How fast must an LP move on a co-underwriting?
→ Often a commitment in three to four weeks, with no guarantee the GP wins the deal. [5]

**23. [Timelines · Hard]** Why is speed itself a selection pressure?
→ The best deals go to LPs who can move; slow LPs get the leftovers, turning slowness into self-inflicted adverse selection.

**24. [Concentration/J-curve · Easy]** What is the J-curve?
→ The early-life dip in a fund's net return/NAV from fees and undeployed/unrealized capital, recovering into a "J."

**25. [Concentration/J-curve · Medium]** How do co-investments mitigate the J-curve?
→ No annual fee drag and immediate deployment, so capital starts working at once and is not eroded by early fees. [2][6]

**26. [Concentration/J-curve · Hard]** State the diversification math and its lesson.
→ For equal-weighted independent deals, σ_portfolio ≈ σ_deal / √N. Twenty deals cut dispersion to ~22% of one deal's, which is why co-invest outperformance is a portfolio result, not a single-deal one. [8] (Example 5.)

**27. [Concentration/J-curve · Medium]** Does J-curve mitigation reduce risk?
→ No. Faster deployment means faster, concentrated single-asset exposure; you shorten time-to-positive-marks by taking risk sooner.

**28. [Governance · Medium]** What protections does a passive co-investor rely on?
→ Contractual terms in the SPV: information rights, confirmed economics/expense allocation, pari passu treatment, drag/tag rights, follow-on terms, and conflict provisions. [1]

**29. [Governance · Easy]** What does pari passu mean here?
→ The co-investor holds the same security, entry price, and terms as the fund; any divergence is a red flag.

**30. [Underwriting/DD · Hard]** How does co-investment DD differ from primary fund DD?
→ Fund DD is top-down on a manager and a blind pool over weeks to months; co-invest DD is bottom-up on a single named asset, in days to weeks, on the GP's clock, with heavy reliance on the GP's specific diligence and an extra "why us?" question. [5]

**31. [Underwriting/DD · Medium]** What are the six underwriting lenses for a single co-investment?
→ Market, company/business quality, management, financials/model, deal structure, and downside/risk.

**32. [Underwriting/DD · Medium]** What is a value-creation bridge and why does it matter?
→ A decomposition of equity return into EBITDA growth, margin expansion, multiple change, and deleveraging. A thesis leaning on multiple expansion is fragile; one driven by EBITDA growth and debt paydown is sturdier.

**33. [IC Memo · Medium]** What section does a co-invest IC memo contain that a fund memo does not?
→ A "why is this deal being offered to us?" / adverse-selection section assessing the GP's motive and alignment.

**34. [Red Flags · Medium]** List four co-investment red flags.
→ No clean reason for sharing the deal; weak GP/fund alignment or non-pari-passu terms; thesis driven by multiple expansion; and a "do every deal" posture, especially heavy deployment in a hot market. [5][6]

**35. [Red Flags · Hard]** Why is "we'll do every co-investment the GP offers" dangerous?
→ It loads the LP into whatever the market overproduces (large, high-multiple deals at cycle peaks) and doubles down on existing fund exposures, exactly the conditions under which co-investments have historically underperformed. [5][6][7]

## Part 18 — Interview questions with model answers

**Q1. What is a co-investment and why would an LP do one?**
A co-investment is a direct minority stake in a single company taken alongside a GP's fund but outside the main fund vehicle, usually with no or reduced fee and carry. [1] LPs do them mainly for net-return enhancement, since the no-fee/no-carry economics preserve gross returns, and secondarily for faster deployment, exposure selectivity, transparency into a named asset, and J-curve mitigation. [2][10] I would add that demand structurally exceeds supply: a StepStone survey cited by Bain found only about half of LPs with appetite were able to participate, which is why co-investment access is a relationship currency GPs ration. [4]

**Q2. Why would a GP give away economics by offering no-fee co-investment?**
Not generosity. The GP needs equity it will not or cannot supply from the fund: the deal is larger than the fund's check, or it would breach the fund's concentration limit, or the GP wants speed and certainty of capital in a competitive process. [1][4] It also uses co-investment to reward its largest LPs and win their next-fund commitments. [1] The flip side I always keep in mind is that the GP has decided not to deploy more fund capital, which I need to explain benignly before I invest.

**Q3. Walk me through how co-investment underwriting differs from underwriting a primary fund commitment.**
Fund DD is top-down: I judge a manager and a strategy for a blind pool, over weeks to months, focusing on team, track record, process, alignment, and terms, with diversification built in. Co-investment DD is bottom-up on one named asset: market, company, management, financials, structure, downside, in days to weeks, on the GP's clock, relying heavily on and re-checking the GP's specific diligence, with no diversification at the deal level. [5] The question that exists only in co-investment is "why is this being offered to me?" Forgetting to ask it is how LPs get adversely selected.

**Q4. Explain adverse selection and tell me whether the evidence supports it.**
Adverse selection is the risk that the deals a GP shares are systematically worse than the ones it keeps, because the GP knows more and chooses what to offer. [7] The evidence is genuinely split. Fang, Ivashina and Lerner (2015) found co-investments underperformed the funds they were made alongside, consistent with adverse selection, while solo deals outperformed. [7] Braun, Jenkinson and Schemmerl (2020), with a larger, selection-bias-free sample, found no adverse selection and that diversified co-investment portfolios beat funds net of fees. [8] I reconcile them by noting different samples and that both agree fee savings help net returns; the real drivers of dispersion are timing and concentration. My takeaway is that adverse selection is a manageable risk, addressed through selectivity, GP alignment checks, avoiding hot-market over-deployment, and diversification, not a reason to avoid co-investing. [5][8]

**Q5. Quantify the fee benefit of co-investing.**
On a deal exiting at 2.5x gross with 8% hurdle, full catch-up, and 20% carry, the fund LP nets 2.20x while the co-investor keeps 2.50x, a 0.30x or roughly 14% uplift from carry alone. At the fund level, adding management fees takes a 2.5x gross to about 1.9x net, so 2-and-20 consumes around 24% of the gross multiple. The cleanest way to think about it is a fee cushion: a co-investment can underperform the fund's deals by 12% to 24% on a gross basis and still match it net. That cushion is the lens for the adverse-selection debate. (Examples 1, 2, 4.)

**Q6. Difference between syndicated and co-underwritten co-investments?**
Syndicated co-investment is offered after the GP has signed the deal: execution-de-risked but more competed. Co-underwriting is offered while the GP is still competing for the asset, with diligence shared in real time, the fastest timelines, and no certainty the GP even wins. [5][10] Co-underwriting demands more from the LP but tends to deliver better access and returns: StepStone data show pre-signing deals at ~2.7x gross TVPI versus ~2.2x post-signing, and GPs increasingly favor LPs who can co-underwrite. [10]

**Q7. How would you underwrite a single co-investment? Give me your framework.**
Six lenses. Market: size, growth, competitive position, where we are in the cycle. Company: business model, unit economics, revenue quality, margins, cash conversion. Management: quality, alignment, track record. Financials: historicals, the base-case model, leverage and covenants, and a value-creation bridge splitting return into EBITDA growth, margin, multiple, and deleveraging. Deal structure: entry valuation versus comps, pari passu treatment, the cap table, and confirmed economics. Downside: an explicit loss case, break-even, leverage stress, exit-multiple sensitivity, and portfolio-concentration fit. Throughout, I keep asking why the GP is sharing this and whether the answer survives scrutiny.

**Q8. A GP offers you a deal at the same entry price as the fund, with the GP putting 25% of its fund into it and management rolling significant equity. The thesis is two-thirds EBITDA growth and deleveraging, one-third multiple expansion, with 4.5x leverage. What do you focus on?**
The alignment signals are positive: pari passu pricing, real fund and management skin in the game. I would press on the model's EBITDA-growth assumptions, since that is the largest return driver and the one most likely to be optimistic; on the durability of margins; and on the 4.5x leverage, specifically maturities, fixed vs floating, covenant headroom, and interest coverage in a downturn. I would sensitize the exit multiple, since multiple expansion is a third of the thesis and is the most fragile leg. Then I would check portfolio fit to make sure I am not doubling down on an exposure I already hold through the fund.

**Q9. What are the biggest risks in co-investing and how do you manage them?**
Adverse selection, single-asset concentration, leverage/financing risk, execution risk on co-underwriting (the deal may die), and timing risk (over-deploying in frothy markets). I manage them with deal-by-deal selectivity and a real "why us" assessment, per-deal sizing limits and diversification across enough deals and sectors and vintages that the √N effect works, explicit leverage and downside stress testing, disciplined pacing across the cycle, and the willingness to give a fast no when the timeline does not allow real diligence. [5][8]

**Q10. How do co-investments affect the J-curve?**
They flatten and shorten it. A fund's early net NAV dips below cost because it pays management fees and deploys into companies that have not yet appreciated. A co-investment pays no annual fee and deploys immediately, so it starts at cost and climbs sooner. [2][6] But I would caution that mitigating the J-curve is not reducing risk: faster deployment is faster, concentrated exposure.

**Q11. What governance and information rights does a co-investor get?**
Typically none of control: no board seat, passive minority. [1] Protections are contractual in the SPV: information and reporting rights, confirmed no-fee/no-carry economics and expense allocation, pari passu treatment, drag-along and tag-along rights, follow-on terms, and conflict provisions. The reference for best practice is ILPA, which emphasizes alignment, transparency, and allocating suitable opportunities to the fund first. [3]

**Q12. What goes into a co-investment IC memo, and what makes it different from a fund memo?**
An executive summary with the recommendation and headline returns, a transaction overview, the GP/sponsor assessment, the six diligence lenses, valuation and returns with base/upside/downside and sensitivities, risks and mitigants with an explicit downside case, and portfolio fit. The section a fund memo lacks is "why is this being offered to us?", an honest assessment of the GP's motive and alignment, because that is where adverse selection lives.

**Q13. If you could ask a GP only three questions before co-investing, what would they be?**
Why are you sharing this deal rather than putting more fund capital in? How much of the fund's and the GP's own capital, and management's, is in the deal, and are we pari passu? And what does your downside case look like, including the leverage under stress and the break-even? Those three get at motive, alignment, and risk faster than anything else.

**Q14. When would you pass on a co-investment even if you like the company?**
When the GP cannot give a clean reason for sharing it, when alignment is thin or terms are not pari passu, when the thesis leans on multiple expansion or an aggressive exit multiple, when leverage will not survive a downturn, when it doubles down on an exposure I already hold, or when the timeline makes genuine diligence impossible. Liking the company is necessary but not sufficient; the deal, the price, the alignment, and the portfolio fit all have to clear. [5][6]

## Part 19 — Sources and where they disagree

**Where sources genuinely disagree:** the central, unresolved dispute is whether co-investments suffer from adverse selection. **Fang, Ivashina and Lerner (2015) say yes** (co-investments underperformed the funds they were made alongside) [7]; **Braun, Jenkinson and Schemmerl (2020) say no** (gross parity, and net outperformance for diversified portfolios) [8]. The disagreement stems from different samples (specific institutions' realized choices vs the full deal universe of funds) and methods, and from gross-vs-net framing. Practitioner sources (Cambridge Associates [6], Adams Street [9]) lean toward "no adverse selection," but Cambridge's headline comparison is gross-co-invest vs net-fund (which flatters co-investments) [6], and Adams Street is an interested party (a co-investment manager) [9]. Treat any single source's verdict with skepticism and reason from the fee cushion (Example 4).
