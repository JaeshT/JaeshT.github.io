# The Current PE Market and Secondaries: A Study Guide for a Primaries and Co-Investments Team
### State of the market, 2025 into mid-June 2026

**Audience:** an incoming intern on an LP-side Primaries and Co-Investments team that sits adjacent to a secondaries desk.
**Goal:** give you the macro picture, the secondaries machinery, the NAV-financing fight, and the co-investment economics at a level of rigor you can defend in an interview and reuse on the desk.
**As of date:** June 8, 2026. Full-year 2025 data comes from the 2026 editions of the major reports (Bain, McKinsey, Jefferies), which were published February and March 2026.

**A note on accuracy.** Where authoritative sources disagree on a number, this guide flags the disagreement rather than papering over it. The secondaries market in particular has no single official scorekeeper, so volume figures vary by advisor. Treat round numbers as estimates.

# PART 1 - The macro backdrop

## 1.1 The one-sentence version

Interest rates rose hard in 2022 to 2023, stayed high into 2024, and have only partly come back down; exits stalled for three years, so cash returned to investors (DPI) collapsed to record lows; and that liquidity drought is the single force driving almost everything else in this guide, including the secondaries boom, the NAV-lending fight, and the rise of co-investment.

## 1.2 Interest rates: the path that matters

The federal funds target range peaked at **5.25% to 5.50%**, held from July 2023 to September 2024 (Statista, citing the Federal Reserve). That was the highest policy rate in over two decades and it is the root cause of the slowdown that followed.

The path since:
- **2024:** the Fed cut three times (50 basis points in September, 25 in November, 25 in December), ending 2024 at **4.25% to 4.50%** (CRS, Congress.gov; Gulf News, Dec 2024).
- **2025:** the Fed held for the first nine months, then cut at each of the three final meetings of the year (September, October, December), ending 2025 at **3.50% to 3.75%**. The December 2025 cut passed on a 10 to 3 vote, the first three-way split since 2019 (Chase; Gulf News; Statista; CRS).
- **2026 so far:** the Fed has held at **3.50% to 3.75%** at the January, March, and April meetings. The hold reflects a renewed inflation scare: a Middle East oil shock pushed April 2026 CPI to **3.8% year over year**, the highest reading since 2023, with energy up sharply. Unemployment sits around **4.3% to 4.4%** (Federal Reserve FOMC minutes, April 2026; Polymarket market-implied odds, June 2026). Jerome Powell's term as Chair expired May 15, 2026; Kevin Hassett has been reported as the frontrunner to succeed him, and the succession adds policy uncertainty.

**Why rates matter so much to PE.** Leveraged buyouts are financed with debt. Higher rates raise the cost of that debt, compress the price a sponsor can pay, and slow refinancing. They also raise the discount rate on future cash flows, which pressures entry and exit multiples. McKinsey's blunt framing: the conditions that amplified PE returns for a decade, namely declining rates, expanding multiples, and abundant leverage, "have passed" (McKinsey, Global Private Markets Report 2026). McKinsey estimates that **leverage and multiple expansion together accounted for 59% of buyout returns between 2010 and 2022**. With those tailwinds gone, returns now have to come from operations.

## 1.3 The exit drought and the liquidity squeeze

For three straight years (2022 to 2024), it was hard to sell portfolio companies at attractive prices. M&A was slow, the IPO window was mostly shut, and sponsor-to-sponsor sales dried up because buyers faced the same expensive debt. The result is a backlog:

- The industry is sitting on roughly **32,000 unsold companies worth about $3.8 trillion**, with the average holding period at exit around seven years (Bain, Global Private Equity Report 2026).
- McKinsey counts **more than 16,000 companies held longer than four years, equal to 52% of all buyout-backed inventory as of 2025, the highest share on record**, with the typical portfolio company now held more than 6.5 years (McKinsey 2026).

**2025 was a partial thaw, but a narrow one.** Bain calls it a "K-shaped recovery":
- Buyout deal value rose **44% to $904 billion** in 2025; exit value jumped **47% to $717 billion**. Both were the second-highest totals on record, behind only 2021 (Bain 2026).
- But the rebound was concentrated at the top. **Just 13 deals above $10 billion accounted for $274 billion of the global gain, and 11 of those were in the US** (Bain 2026). Headline megadeals included the **$56.6 billion take-private of Electronic Arts** (a new buyout record), **Aligned Data Centers at $40 billion**, **Air Lease at $27.5 billion**, and **Walgreens Boots Alliance at $23.7 billion** (Bain 2026).
- IPOs rose 36% off a very low base but remained a minor exit route; sponsor-to-sponsor exits grew 21% globally (Bain 2026).

The recovery helped the headline numbers but did little for the core problem, which is cash back to investors.

## 1.4 DPI and the liquidity problem (the single most important theme)

**DPI (distributions to paid-in capital)** measures how much cash a fund has actually returned relative to what investors paid in. It is the metric LPs now care about most, because unrealized gains do not pay pension benefits or fund endowment spending.

The numbers are stark:
- Bain: **distributions as a percentage of NAV have stayed below 15% for four years running, an industry record low** (Bain 2026).
- McKinsey: **distributions to paid-in as a share of total PE AUM were just 6% in the twelve months ended June 2025, versus a 2015 to 2019 average of 16%** (McKinsey 2026, via TEXPERS summary).
- McKinsey also reports that in its January 2025 LP survey, **2.5 times as many LPs ranked DPI as a "most critical" metric compared with three years earlier** (McKinsey, Global Private Markets Report 2025).

This is the engine behind the rest of the guide. When distributions stall, LPs cannot recycle cash into new commitments, they hit the denominator effect (see below), and they reach for any tool that produces liquidity. Those tools are exactly the secondaries, continuation vehicles, NAV loans, and co-investment economics covered in Parts 4 through 8.

## 1.5 The denominator effect

When public markets fall, an LP's public holdings shrink in value while its private holdings, which are marked less frequently, stay roughly flat. The private allocation, measured as a percentage of the total portfolio, then mechanically rises above target. An LP with a 10% private-equity target can suddenly find itself at 13% without buying anything, simply because the denominator (total portfolio value) fell. That pushes some LPs to sell private stakes (an LP-led secondary) to rebalance, or to slow new commitments. The effect was acute in 2022 and remains a live consideration whenever public equities wobble.

## 1.6 Valuations

Entry multiples are high by historical standards, which McKinsey cites as one reason recent-vintage returns are weak ("paying more"). On the secondary market, the relevant valuation question is the **discount to NAV** at which fund stakes trade, covered in Part 5. The short version: in 2025, average LP-stake pricing was 87% of NAV, meaning a typical 13% discount, with wide variation by strategy and fund age.

# PART 2 - Fundraising climate and dry powder

## 2.1 Fundraising: four years in the doldrums

Primary fundraising (LPs committing to new blind-pool funds) has declined for four consecutive years and 2025 did not break the trend. Exact figures depend on scope and data provider, so several measures are given below.

- **PitchBook:** overall private-capital fundraising fell **13.3% in 2025**, with fund counts dropping sharply across strategies. Real assets and secondaries were the exceptions, posting year-over-year gains (PitchBook, 2025 Annual Global Private Market Fundraising Report, March 2026).
- **Preqin (via CEPR):** PE across all strategies including venture was on track to raise under **$600 billion in 2025** ($502.1 billion through Q3), versus $664.8 billion in 2024, a 2023 peak of $840.9 billion, and $734.5 billion in 2019 (CEPR, Jan 2026, citing Preqin).
- **PitchBook (trailing-twelve-month, late 2025):** buyout/PE fundraising of $440 billion was down 32.3% year over year; venture $140.4 billion (down 42.5%); private credit $218 billion (down ~17%). The bright spots were **secondaries at $122.6 billion (up 22%)** and co-investment funds at $43.5 billion (Institutional Investor, Dec 2025, citing PitchBook).

**Source disagreement to flag:** the headline "decline" ranges from roughly 13% (PitchBook, all private capital, full-year 2025) to over 30% (PitchBook PE-only, trailing twelve months) depending on the window and the asset-class scope. They are not contradictory; they measure different things. Use the all-private-capital figure (down ~13% in 2025) for the broad statement and the PE-only figure for the buyout-specific one.

**Structural shifts inside fundraising:**
- **Scale is winning.** Larger sponsors capture a growing share; funds under $500 million shrank to about 13% of fundraising, and GP consolidation (firms buying other firms) doubled to about $34 billion (McKinsey 2026, via secondary summaries).
- **Regional split (2025, McKinsey private-markets basis):** North America up 8% to $432 billion; Europe down 41% to $118 billion (largely a base effect, since big European funds closed in 2023 to 2024); Asia-Pacific down 49% to $49 billion.
- **Europe gained share** on a commitments basis, capturing a record 34% of global commitments in Q3 2025, driven by infrastructure and secondaries (PitchBook, Q3 2025).
- **The retail and semi-liquid wave.** US fundraising into semi-liquid/retail PE vehicles reached **$204 billion in 2025, more than double the $92 billion of 2023** (McKinsey 2026, citing Robert A. Stanger & Co.). This is the "democratization" of private markets and is one of the few areas of clear growth.
- **Alternative AUM** across all forms grew roughly 10% to 15% in 2025, from about $8 trillion to $8.5 trillion, outpacing traditional closed-end commingled AUM, which grew 5% to 10% to $16 trillion to $16.5 trillion (McKinsey 2026). Custom options like separately managed accounts and side deals are the fastest-growing category.

## 2.2 LP conviction is intact, but the terms have shifted

Despite weak distributions, LPs are not abandoning the asset class. In McKinsey's January 2026 survey of 300 global LPs, **about 70% planned to maintain or increase PE allocations in 2026** (McKinsey 2026). But LPs are using their leverage: the ILPA LP Sentiment Survey 2025 to 2026 edition found that **more than half of LPs believe they have more negotiating leverage with GPs than they did twelve months earlier** (Bain 2026, citing ILPA). That leverage shows up as fee concessions and, above all, demands for co-investment (Part 8).

## 2.3 Dry powder: large, aging, and contested in size

**Dry powder** is committed capital that has been raised but not yet invested. It matters because aging dry powder pressures GPs to deploy before investment periods expire, which supports deal activity even in a soft market.

The level depends entirely on what you count:
- **Buyout dry powder: about $1.3 trillion**, with the majority sitting in 2022 to 2023 vintages and therefore aging (Bain 2026).
- **All private-equity dry powder: about $2.18 trillion** as of March 31, 2025, down 5.2% from the December 2023 record of $2.305 trillion (S&P Global Market Intelligence, citing Preqin, Dec 2025). Venture dry powder was $600.9 billion, down 19% from its 2023 peak.
- **All closed-end private capital dry powder: $4.63 trillion** at the end of Q2 2025, up 4.6% from year-end 2024, with PE accounting for roughly 97% of the increase (PitchBook, Jan 2026).

**Source disagreement to flag:** these three figures ($1.3T, $2.18T, $4.63T) are not in conflict; they nest. The smallest is buyout-only (Bain), the middle is all PE including growth and venture (Preqin), and the largest is all private capital including credit, real assets, and infrastructure (PitchBook). Always state the scope when you quote a dry-powder number.

A key qualifier from Bain: the $1.3 trillion buyout pile had a "relatively modest" impact on 2025's megadeals because much of the equity in those deals came from outside PE funds, especially sovereign wealth funds and corporates. So the money chasing deals is even larger than the dry-powder figure suggests, and PE's share of large-deal equity is being competed away (Bain 2026).

# PART 3 - Performance and the returns debate

## 3.1 The uncomfortable comparison

The most-cited and most-pointed finding of the 2026 reports is that PE has lagged public markets:

- **In 2025, top-quartile global buyout returns averaged 8% on a pooled IRR basis, less than half the S&P 500's 18% and MSCI World's 22%** (McKinsey 2026, verbatim from the primary report). Overall buyout returns in 2025 were approximately 7%, the third consecutive year of underperformance versus US and global public equities.
- **Buyout fund IRRs hit a post-2002 trough between 2022 and 2025, averaging 5.7% pooled** (McKinsey 2026).
- **The 2015 to 2017 vintages are generating only about 2% IRRs**, dragging the 2015 to 2025 average buyout return to roughly 6%, even though newer (and largely unrealized) vintages are showing about 15% IRR (McKinsey 2026).

## 3.2 The rebuttal: the long-horizon and top-quartile case

The bear case above is real but incomplete. The defenders of PE point out:

- **Over the past decade, top-quartile buyout funds generated about 24% IRR, beating the S&P 500's 15% and MSCI World's 13% total shareholder return** (McKinsey 2026). The asset class's value has always been concentrated in the top managers, and dispersion is the point, not a bug.
- **Hamilton Lane** estimates $1 invested in private equity in 2015 grew to $3.96 by 2024, versus $3.51 for the S&P 500 and $2.61 for MSCI World (Hamilton Lane, 2025 Market Overview). Long-horizon PME comparisons still favor PE in most rolling windows.
- The weak recent numbers are heavily driven by **unrealized marks and the realization drought**, not by capital losses. Almost every private-markets vintage in the last decade has delivered positive, usually double-digit, pooled IRRs (Hamilton Lane).

**The synthesis for an LP team:** manager selection has never mattered more. With the macro tailwinds gone, the spread between top-quartile and median funds is what determines whether PE earns its illiquidity premium and fee load. This is precisely why a primaries team's job is underwriting GPs, not just buying the asset class.

## 3.3 Why IRR alone is misleading (and why DPI rose to dominance)

Two reasons IRR can flatter a fund:
1. **Subscription lines (capital-call facilities)** let a GP delay calling LP capital by borrowing at the fund level first. Because IRR is time-sensitive, delaying the capital call shortens the period capital is outstanding and can inflate reported IRR by an estimated 200 to 500 basis points without changing the cash ultimately returned (Preqin, via industry analysis). DPI and MOIC are unaffected by this trick, which is one reason LPs migrated to DPI.
2. **Unrealized IRR is not cash.** A fund can report a 35% IRR with a DPI of 0.3x (lots of paper gains, little cash back) while another reports 11% IRR with 1.9x DPI (less paper gain, far more cash). For an LP paying real obligations, the second fund is better. This "IRR-DPI disconnect" is the conceptual heart of today's liquidity debate.

# PART 4 - Secondaries fundamentals (the part adjacent to your desk)

## 4.1 What a secondary is, and why the market exists

A primary commitment is when an LP commits capital to a brand-new, blind-pool fund and funds it over time as the GP calls capital. A **secondary** is a transaction in an **existing** fund interest or portfolio of interests, after the fund is already partway through its life. The buyer steps into the seller's shoes: they take over the seller's remaining unfunded commitment and receive the seller's share of future distributions.

The market exists because primary fund interests are illiquid and locked up for ten years or more, but investors' circumstances change. Reasons a holder sells include: needing liquidity, rebalancing away from an overweight (the denominator effect), exiting a GP relationship they no longer want to re-up with, cleaning up a portfolio of tail-end funds, or a regulatory or strategic shift. The secondary market converts a ten-year illiquid commitment into something that can be sold in months. It has grown from a niche, distressed-seller corner (about $26 billion in 2013) into a core portfolio-management tool measured in the hundreds of billions (Augment/Chronograph; Jefferies).

The two halves of the market:
- **LP-led** (also "LP secondaries" or "traditional secondaries"): an LP sells its fund interest(s) to a buyer. The GP is generally a bystander who only has to approve the transfer.
- **GP-led**: the GP itself initiates a transaction to move one or more assets out of an existing fund, most commonly into a **continuation vehicle** (Part 6). Here the GP is the driver, not a bystander.

## 4.2 How an LP-led deal works

1. **Seller decides to sell** one fund stake or a portfolio of stakes.
2. **An advisor (intermediary)** such as Evercore, Jefferies, Lazard, PJT Park Hill, Campbell Lutyens, or Greenhill runs a process, prepares data, and contacts buyers.
3. **Buyers diligence the underlying assets.** Because a fund interest is a claim on a portfolio of companies, the buyer is really underwriting the remaining portfolio company by company, projecting future distributions, and forming a view on NAV accuracy.
4. **Buyers bid a price as a percentage of NAV**, usually referencing a recent quarter's reported NAV (the "reference date"), with an adjustment for cash flows between the reference date and closing.
5. **The winning bidder and seller sign a purchase agreement**; the GP consents to the transfer; the interest changes hands.

The buyer pays the price for the existing NAV and also assumes the **unfunded commitment** (the capital the GP has not yet called). So the total exposure a buyer takes on is larger than the headline purchase price.

## 4.3 How GP-led deals work (brief here, detailed in Part 6)

The GP sets up a new vehicle, sells one or more assets from the old fund into it at a negotiated price, brings in new secondary buyers to fund the purchase, and offers existing LPs a choice: take cash now (sell) or **roll** their interest into the new vehicle and stay invested. The GP keeps managing the assets and earns fees on the new vehicle. This is why GP-leds carry a structural conflict of interest, addressed in Part 6.

## 4.4 Pricing: discount and premium to NAV

The central number in any secondary is the **price as a percentage of NAV**.

- **Price as % of NAV = Purchase price / Reported NAV.**
- **Discount to NAV = 1 - (Price / NAV).** A stake bought at 90% of NAV trades at a 10% discount.
- A price above 100% of NAV is a **premium**, which happens for high-quality, recent-vintage buyout or hot-sector funds where buyers expect NAV to be marked conservatively.

**Why stakes trade at a discount.** Several reasons stack:
1. **Time value and the buyer's required return.** A buyer wants a target net IRR (often mid-teens or higher). Paying less than NAV today is how they build in return, since the assets are expected to be worth NAV (or more) but only realized over several years.
2. **NAV staleness and conservatism.** Reported NAV is usually a quarter or two old. Buyers discount for the risk that marks are stale or optimistic.
3. **Illiquidity and information asymmetry.** The seller usually knows the assets better than the buyer.
4. **The unfunded commitment.** The buyer must reserve capital to fund future calls, which has a cost.
5. **Strategy and vintage risk.** Venture and tail-end funds carry more uncertainty, so they trade cheaper.

**Why a rational LP sells at a discount anyway.** Selling at, say, 90% of NAV looks like leaving 10% on the table. But the seller gets certain cash today instead of an uncertain stream over five to seven years, removes the unfunded commitment and its drawdown risk, frees capital to redeploy or to meet obligations, and avoids the administrative drag of tail-end funds. For an LP facing the denominator effect or a cash need, that certainty is worth the discount. This trade-off (certain liquidity now versus uncertain higher value later) is the single most important intuition in LP secondaries.

## 4.5 Deferred consideration and structuring

Buyers increasingly use **deferred purchase price**: they pay part of the price at closing and the rest later (often 12 to 24 months out), sometimes interest-free. Deferral improves the buyer's IRR (less capital out earlier) and lets them bid a higher headline price, which helps the seller's optics. In 2025, deferred pricing was used in roughly **23% of LP transactions and about 29% of GP-led transaction volume** (Jefferies, 2025 Global Secondary Market Review). Other structures include preferred equity (a financing where the provider gets a priority claim on distributions up to a return cap, leaving upside with the holder) and strip sales (selling a "strip," or a pro-rata slice, of many positions).

# PART 5 - The secondaries boom: 2025 data

## 5.1 Record volume

2025 was the largest year on record for secondaries.

- **Total secondary volume reached about $240 billion in 2025, up roughly 48% year over year, a record** (Jefferies, 2025 Global Secondary Market Review, Feb 2026; McKinsey 2026 reports the identical $240 billion and +48%). For reference, 2024 was about $162 billion.
- **GP-led volume was about $115 billion, up 53%, accounting for 48% of the total** (Jefferies). Lazard reported a near-identical $116 billion (Lazard, Secondary Market Report 2025).
- **LP-led volume was therefore about $125 billion, roughly 52% of the market.** Deal sizes scaled up: 27 LP deals exceeded $1 billion and the largest LP transaction topped $5 billion (Jefferies).

**Source disagreement to flag:** Jefferies and McKinsey both put 2025 growth at 48% to $240 billion. **Bain reports secondary volume up only 41%** (Bain 2026), a lower figure that likely reflects a different scope or base. Some commentary cited a $226 billion figure (Augment, citing a different provider). For the canonical statement, use $240 billion and +48% (Jefferies/McKinsey consensus) and note Bain's 41% as the dissenting figure.

## 5.2 Pricing in 2025

- **Average LP portfolio price finished 2025 at 87% of NAV, down about 200 basis points from 2024** (Jefferies). The decline reflected an older vintage mix of buyout portfolios brought to market (average vintage 2016 versus 2018 the prior year) and a larger share of venture and growth deals, which price lower.
- **By strategy (LP pricing, 2025):** buyout 92% of NAV; venture and growth 78%; credit 91% (stable); real estate 70% (Jefferies).
- **By fund age:** funds less than five years old priced at about 95% of NAV; tail-end funds older than ten years priced at about 73% of NAV (Jefferies). Newer, higher-quality funds trade near or above par; old tails trade at deep discounts.

## 5.3 Why the boom happened

The boom is the liquidity drought's release valve. With M&A and IPOs slow and DPI at record lows, LPs turned to the secondary market to manufacture their own liquidity, and GPs turned to continuation vehicles to return cash to LPs without fire-selling assets. Secondaries fundraising hit records as buyers raised capital to meet the supply: secondaries funds called about $83.4 billion in 2025, the highest annual tally on record, with about $50.6 billion called in the first half alone (PitchBook). One structural constraint remains: buyers argue the market is under-capitalized relative to potential supply, which keeps a lid on prices (Evercore commentary, 2025).

A notable feature of 2025 supply: about 40% of LP-led sellers in the first half were first-time sellers (Evercore H1 2025, via Future Standard), meaning the stigma of selling has largely disappeared. Selling is now active portfolio management, not a distress signal.

# PART 6 - GP-led secondaries and continuation vehicles (deep dive)

## 6.1 What a continuation vehicle is

A **continuation vehicle (CV)** or **continuation fund** is a new fund, raised and managed by the same GP, created to buy one or more assets out of an older fund whose life is ending. The purpose is to extend the holding period on assets the GP wants to keep, while giving the original fund's LPs a choice between cash and continued exposure.

- A **single-asset CV** holds one company (often a "trophy" asset the GP does not want to sell at a depressed multiple).
- A **multi-asset CV** holds several companies.

In 2025, continuation funds dominated GP-leds: Lazard reported single- and multi-asset CVs were **77% of GP-led deals**; other commentary puts CVs at roughly **90% of GP-led volume** (Lazard; ABF Journal). The discrepancy is a definitional one (whether you count preferred-equity and strip-sale GP-leds separately). Either way, CVs are the dominant form.

## 6.2 The mechanics, step by step

1. **The GP identifies an asset** (or assets) to move, typically a strong performer it believes has more room to run.
2. **An independent valuation and a fairness opinion** are obtained to set the transfer price.
3. **The GP forms the CV** and runs a process to bring in new secondary buyers who provide the cash.
4. **Existing LPs elect**: take cash now at the transaction price (a "sell" or "cash-out"), or **roll** their interest into the CV and stay invested. Ideally they also get a **status quo option**, the ability to roll on the same economic terms as the original fund rather than the CV's new (usually more expensive) terms.
5. **The legacy fund is paid**, the cash-electing LPs receive their money, and the GP continues managing the asset inside the CV, earning fresh fees.

The **status quo option** matters because, without it, a rolling LP faces a forced binary: cash out, or roll into a vehicle with reset, often higher, fees and carry. ILPA recommends giving LPs a status quo option and a meaningful election period of **at least 20 business days** to decide (ILPA continuation-fund guidance, 2023; Pipeline Road summary).

## 6.3 The conflict of interest (the heart of the GP-led debate)

A CV creates a structural conflict because **the GP is on both sides of the trade**. It is the fiduciary for the selling fund (which wants a high price) and simultaneously the manager of the buying CV (which wants a low price), and it sets the price for an asset it is selling to itself (CFA Institute, "Continuation Funds: Ethics in Private Markets," September 2025; Carta; Norton Rose Fulbright).

The GP's incentives to launch a CV are not neutral:
- **Prolonged management fees** on a fresh vehicle's NAV.
- **Carry crystallization**: the "sale" can trigger carried interest on the asset from the old fund, even though the GP is effectively keeping the investment.
- **Reset economics and a larger equity stake** in an asset the GP believes is a winner.

Governance mechanisms exist to police the conflict: a **conflict-of-interest waiver from the legacy fund's LPAC** (LP Advisory Committee), an independent fairness opinion, independent valuation shared before LPs elect, full disclosure of all fees and carry resets, and the status quo option (ILPA 2023; CFA Institute 2025). The **SEC's 2025 examination priorities specifically flagged disclosure of conflicts in continuation-fund transactions** as a focus area (ABF Journal, citing SEC; the broader Private Fund Adviser Rules that would have mandated fairness opinions were vacated by the Fifth Circuit in 2024, so the current regime is guidance and exam pressure rather than hard rule).

## 6.4 The reputation shift

CVs used to carry a stigma. A decade ago, "GP-led restructurings" were how managers quarantined struggling assets and bought more time, so a GP-led signaled a problem fund (Aztec Group). That has flipped. Today CVs are mostly used for **trophy assets**, are seen as legitimate portfolio management, and have spread into private credit (credit GP-led volume more than tripled in 2025 and GP-leds became the majority of credit secondary volume for the first time) and infrastructure, where 84% of LPs reported seeing more infrastructure CVs come to market (Jefferies; Infrastructure Investor LP Perspectives Study 2025). Technology was the dominant CV sector, at about 24% of GP-led deal value in H1 2025 (ABF Journal).

## 6.5 What an LP team underwrites in a GP-led

When your team is offered a roll-or-sell decision (as an LP in the legacy fund) or a chance to invest in the CV as a buyer, the questions are: Is the transfer price fair and independently supported? Are the CV's terms (fee, carry, hurdle) reasonable versus the legacy fund? Is there a status quo option? Why is the GP keeping this asset rather than selling it (genuine upside, or fee extension)? How aligned is the GP, measured by how much of its own crystallized carry it is rolling back into the CV? Alignment, meaning the GP reinvesting its proceeds into the CV, is the strongest signal that the deal is about upside and not just fees.

# PART 7 - NAV financing and the NAV-lending controversy

## 7.1 What a NAV facility is

A **NAV-based facility (NAV loan)** is a loan to a fund that is secured by the value of the fund's investment portfolio (its NAV), rather than by the uncalled commitments of its LPs. It sits at the fund level, on top of any leverage at the individual portfolio companies. ILPA defines it as a credit facility backed by substantially all of the fund's assets or its rights to distributions (ILPA, 2024; White & Case).

Contrast it with a **subscription line** (a "sub line" or capital-call facility), which is secured by LPs' unfunded commitments and is short-term bridge financing used early in a fund's life. NAV loans are used later, when commitments are largely drawn and the fund holds a mature portfolio.

## 7.2 What NAV loans are used for

According to ILPA and the Fund Finance Association, roughly **80% of NAV facilities support portfolio companies** (follow-on investments, add-on acquisitions, right-sizing portfolio-company leverage, working capital, currency hedging), and roughly **20% are used to fund distributions to LPs** (White & Case, citing ILPA/FFA). The 20% bucket is where almost all the controversy lives.

## 7.3 Market size and growth

The NAV-lending market was estimated at about **$100 billion currently and is projected by the Fund Finance Association to reach about $600 billion by 2030**, a roughly sixfold increase (ILPA; PitchBook; White & Case). It grew out of secondaries, private credit, and real estate, where it has been used for years; its spread into mainstream buyout funds is the newer and more contentious development.

## 7.4 Why it is controversial

The core objection is to using NAV loans to **fund distributions**. The criticisms:
1. **It manufactures DPI without selling anything.** A GP under fundraising pressure can borrow against the portfolio and send LPs cash that looks like a distribution, improving the optics of DPI and helping the next fundraise, while the underlying assets remain unsold. The LP's money is essentially being returned to them as debt secured by their own assets.
2. **Recallable distributions.** Such distributions are often recallable, so LPs can pay interest on a distribution that is later clawed back, a strange and costly outcome (Seyfarth; Proskauer, citing ILPA).
3. **Added leverage and risk.** A NAV loan adds fund-level debt on top of company-level debt, raising the fund's risk profile in a way LPs may not see.
4. **Transparency and consent gaps.** Many older LPAs are silent on NAV facilities, and GPs have sometimes interpreted borrowing limits not to cover holdco-level NAV debt, so LPs often did not know a facility was in place.
5. **Adverse tax effects** for some US taxable investors when facilities fund distributions.

The defenders' rebuttal, which a balanced analyst should give: the overwhelming majority of NAV-loan use (the ~80%) is value-accretive portfolio management, not distribution engineering; LPs imposing blanket bans in side letters are using a "blunt instrument" that also blocks legitimate uses; and the apprehension mirrors the resistance that greeted subscription lines two decades ago, which the market ultimately accepted (Reed Smith; White & Case). Cambridge Associates' Andrea Auerbach summarized the realpolitik: the industry will not ban NAV loans, "the cat is out of the bag," and both sides need to work out an appropriate role for them (Institutional Investor, Dec 2024).

## 7.5 ILPA's guidance

ILPA issued NAV-facility guidance in July to September 2024. It does **not** recommend banning the loans. Its six core recommendations push transparency and governance (ILPA, 2024; Macfarlanes; Katten):
1. If the LPA is silent on NAV financing, the GP should seek **LPAC consent** before putting a facility in place.
2. Even if the LPA authorizes NAV financing, the GP should seek **LPAC approval to use it for distributions**.
3. Any conflicts of interest should be brought to the LPAC.
4. New LPAs should include clear language and guardrails on NAV facilities.
5. LPs should proactively check whether older fund documents have been interpreted to exclude SPV/holdco-level facilities from fund-level borrowing limits.
6. GPs should give all LPs standardized disclosures once a facility is in place.

For an LP team, the practical upshot: NAV financing is now a standard diligence item. You ask whether the fund permits it, for what purposes, with what disclosure, and whether distribution-funding requires LPAC sign-off.

# PART 8 - Co-investments

## 8.1 What a co-investment is

A **co-investment** is a direct investment by an LP into a specific portfolio company, made alongside a GP's fund, outside the main fund's commingled structure. If a GP is buying Company X with its fund, it may offer some LPs the chance to put additional capital directly into Company X. The LP gets concentrated exposure to a single deal it can actually see, rather than blind-pool exposure to a whole fund.

## 8.2 Why LPs want them: the fee math

The headline appeal is cost. Co-investments are typically offered on a **"no fee, no carry"** or reduced-fee, reduced-carry basis. Because an LP pays full management fees (around 1.5% to 2%) and carry (around 20%) on its main fund commitment but little or nothing on the co-invest, blending co-investment into a program **lowers the all-in fee load and lifts net returns** (Akin; Chronograph; Augment). For a large allocator deploying billions, even modest fee savings compound meaningfully over a fund's life.

Beyond cost, co-investments give LPs portfolio-construction control (they can lean into sectors, geographies, or deal types they like rather than accepting the GP's blind-pool mix), deeper relationships and information access with key GPs, and, on average, attractive performance: StepStone data suggests the **majority of co-investment deals outperform their parent funds** (Akin, citing StepStone).

**The catch on "no fee, no carry."** It does not mean free. Sponsors can still extract **portfolio-company-level fees** that hit co-investors: transaction fees often 1% to 3% of purchase price, and monitoring fees often 1% to 3.5% of EBITDA or a fixed $1 million to $10 million per year (Torys LLP). Co-investors who close late may also bear **warehousing or catch-up costs**, their pro-rata share of the carry and interest the main fund incurred while it held the deal before syndicating it. Diligence the actual economics, not the marketing label.

## 8.3 Why GPs offer them

Co-investments are not pure generosity. GPs use them to:
- **Fund deals too large for one fund** without breaching concentration limits.
- **Reward and retain anchor LPs**, making those LPs more likely to commit to the next fund. Co-investment access is increasingly a condition LPs negotiate during primary fundraising, written into side letters.
- **Raise fee-bearing primary capital.** The implicit deal is: commit to my fund (fee-bearing), and I will give you co-investment (low-fee). The 2026 StepStone/Bain GP Survey found GPs offered anywhere from 110 cents of co-investment per dollar of fee-bearing capital down to none, with a **median of 33 cents per dollar, which translates to roughly a 25% reduction in fee revenue** (Bain 2026). That is the price GPs are paying for capital in a hard fundraising market, and LP leverage means the pressure for more concessions is rising.

## 8.4 Scale of demand

- An **Adams Street survey found 88% of LPs intend to allocate up to 20% of their portfolios to co-investments by 2030** (Akin, citing Adams Street).
- Many large LPs already reserve **15% to 30% of their total private-investment allocation** for co-investments (Chronograph).
- Capital raised specifically into co-investment funds was about **$43.5 billion** on a trailing basis in late 2025 (PitchBook), and a separate dataset put dedicated co-investment vehicle capital at a record $33.2 billion in 2024 across just 40 deals, the lowest deal count since 2013, showing demand running ahead of completed supply (Chronograph).

## 8.5 The risks a co-invest team underwrites

Co-investments concentrate risk (one company, not a diversified pool), demand fast turnaround (deal timelines, not fund timelines), and require real underwriting capability rather than just manager selection. Adverse selection is the central worry: is the GP offering this deal because it is genuinely attractive, or because the GP could not or did not want to hold all of it in the fund? The best-positioned LPs have dedicated co-investment teams, explicit allocations, and the speed to say yes inside a GP's timeline (Chronograph). The "no fee, no carry" saving is only real if the deal itself is sound.

# PART 9 - Key formulas

All written explicitly so they can be coded into calculators. PIC = paid-in capital (also called drawn or contributed capital).

**Performance multiples**
- `DPI = Cumulative Distributions / Paid-in Capital`
- `RVPI = Residual NAV / Paid-in Capital`
- `TVPI = (Cumulative Distributions + Residual NAV) / Paid-in Capital`
- Identity: `TVPI = DPI + RVPI`
- `MOIC (gross, deal level) = Total Value Realized and Unrealized / Invested Capital`
- (TVPI is the fund-level, net-of-fees multiple; MOIC is usually the gross, deal-level multiple. They answer the same "how many times my money" question at different levels.)

**IRR**
- IRR is the discount rate r that sets net present value to zero across the dated cash-flow stream:
  `0 = Σ [ CF_t / (1 + r)^t ]` summed over all periods t, where contributions are negative cash flows and distributions plus final NAV are positive.
- IRR is money-weighted and time-sensitive, so the timing of cash flows changes it even if total cash is identical.

**Secondary pricing**
- `Price as % of NAV = Purchase Price / Reference NAV`
- `Discount to NAV = 1 - (Purchase Price / Reference NAV)`
- `Total buyer exposure = Purchase Price + Unfunded Commitment assumed`

**Buyer's expected return on a secondary (simplified, single future exit)**
- `Gross MOIC = Expected Future Value Received / Price Paid`
- If a stake bought at price P returns total value V after n years: `Gross IRR = (V / P)^(1/n) - 1`

**Public Market Equivalent (PME), Kaplan-Schoar version**
- `KS-PME = (Σ Distributions_t / Index_t + Final NAV / Index_T) / (Σ Contributions_t / Index_t)`
- Intuition: discount every fund cash flow by the public index level on its date, as if you had invested the same cash flows into the index. KS-PME above 1.0 means the fund beat the index for the same cash-flow timing; below 1.0 means it lagged. (Kaplan and Schoar, 2005. Related methods: Long-Nickels PME, PME+ by Rouvinez, and Direct Alpha by Gredil, Griffiths, and Stucke, which expresses the result as an annualized excess return.)

**Distribution as a percentage of NAV (the liquidity metric Bain tracks)**
- `Distribution yield = Distributions in period / Beginning NAV`

**Co-investment blended fee**
- `Blended fee rate = (Fund capital × Fund fee + Co-invest capital × Co-invest fee) / (Fund capital + Co-invest capital)`

**Leverage in a buyout (why rates matter)**
- `Equity at entry = Enterprise Value - Net Debt`
- `Equity value at exit = Exit Enterprise Value - Net Debt at exit`
- `Equity MOIC = Equity value at exit / Equity at entry`
- Higher rates raise the cost of the debt, which lowers the EV a sponsor can pay and shrinks the equity return for a given exit.

# PART 10 - Worked numerical examples

Each example shows the actual arithmetic step by step.

## Example 1 - LP-stake pricing and total consideration

A pension fund sells its interest in Buyout Fund VII.
- Reported NAV (reference date): **$100.0 million**
- Unfunded commitment still owed to the GP: **$20.0 million**
- Winning bid: **92% of NAV** (a buyout fund, in line with the 2025 average of 92%)

Step 1. Purchase price for the existing NAV = 0.92 × $100.0m = **$92.0m**.
Step 2. Discount to NAV = 1 − 0.92 = **8%**, i.e. the seller gives up $8.0m of reported value for certain cash today.
Step 3. Total exposure the buyer takes on = purchase price + unfunded = $92.0m + $20.0m = **$112.0m** of future commitment, even though only $92.0m changes hands at closing.

Takeaway: the headline "$92m" understates the buyer's real commitment, because the $20m unfunded will be called over time.

## Example 2 - The buyer's return when the stake is bought at a discount

Continue Example 1. The buyer pays $92.0m for assets reported at $100.0m NAV. Assume the portfolio is realized for **$130.0m total over 4 years** (NAV grows and is distributed), and ignore the unfunded for simplicity.

Step 1. Gross MOIC = $130.0m / $92.0m = **1.41x**.
Step 2. Gross IRR = (130/92)^(1/4) − 1 = (1.4130)^(0.25) − 1.
   (1.4130)^(0.25) = e^(0.25 × ln 1.4130) = e^(0.25 × 0.3457) = e^(0.0864) = 1.0903.
   Gross IRR = **9.0%** per year.
Step 3. Note where the return came from. Of the 1.41x, part is the **8% entry discount** (buying $100 of NAV for $92) and part is the **30% NAV growth** ($100 to $130). If the buyer had paid full NAV ($100m), MOIC would be 1.30x and IRR (130/100)^(0.25) − 1 = **6.8%**. The discount added about 2.2 points of annual IRR.

Takeaway: in secondaries, return is built from two sources, the entry discount and the underlying asset growth. The discount is the lever the buyer controls at purchase.

## Example 3 - Fund-level DPI, RVPI, TVPI

Fund IV has called **$500m** from LPs. It has distributed **$350m** in cash and carries a residual NAV of **$400m**.

Step 1. DPI = $350m / $500m = **0.70x** (70 cents of cash back per dollar in).
Step 2. RVPI = $400m / $500m = **0.80x** (80 cents of unrealized value per dollar in).
Step 3. TVPI = DPI + RVPI = 0.70 + 0.80 = **1.50x** (total value, realized plus paper, of 1.5x).

Takeaway: a 1.5x TVPI looks healthy, but with DPI at only 0.70x the LP has gotten back less than it put in. In today's market, LPs scrutinize the 0.70x far more than the 1.50x.

## Example 4 - The IRR versus DPI disconnect

Two funds, same LP, both five years old.
- **Fund A:** net IRR 35%, DPI 0.3x.
- **Fund B:** net IRR 11%, DPI 1.9x.

On $100m committed and called to each:
- Fund A has returned 0.3 × $100m = **$30m** in cash.
- Fund B has returned 1.9 × $100m = **$190m** in cash.

Takeaway: Fund A wins the quarterly-report beauty contest on IRR, but Fund B has returned more than six times the cash. Pension benefits and endowment spending are paid in cash, not IRR. This is exactly why DPI displaced IRR as the metric LPs rank "most critical." (Illustrative figures in the spirit of the IRR-DPI disconnect; the structure, not the exact numbers, is the lesson.)

## Example 5 - How a subscription line flatters IRR

A fund makes a deal that returns 1.5x over 3 years.
- **Without a sub line:** the GP calls $100m from LPs on day 0. Three years later it returns $150m. LP IRR = (150/100)^(1/3) − 1 = (1.5)^(0.333) − 1 = **14.5%**.
- **With a sub line:** the GP funds the $100m with a credit line for the first year, then calls the $100m from LPs at the start of year 1. LP capital is outstanding for only 2 years before the $150m comes back. LP IRR = (150/100)^(1/2) − 1 = (1.5)^(0.5) − 1 = **22.5%**.

Step 1. Same deal, same 1.5x MOIC, same $150m of cash.
Step 2. IRR jumped from 14.5% to 22.5% purely because LP capital was outstanding for less time.
Step 3. DPI and MOIC are identical in both cases (1.5x). Only IRR moved.

Takeaway: IRR can be engineered by delaying capital calls. DPI and MOIC cannot. (Interest cost on the line is ignored here for clarity; in reality it modestly reduces net proceeds.)

## Example 6 - A NAV loan used to fund a distribution

Fund VI holds a portfolio with NAV of **$1,000m**. It has not sold anything but wants to show DPI before its next fundraise. PIC to date is $800m, distributions so far $80m, so DPI is 0.10x.

The GP draws a NAV loan of **$100m** (10% of NAV) and distributes it to LPs.

Step 1. New cumulative distributions = $80m + $100m = $180m. New DPI = $180m / $800m = **0.225x**, up from 0.10x. Optics improved sharply.
Step 2. But the fund now owes $100m plus interest, secured by the same $1,000m portfolio. Net asset value to LPs after the loan is $1,000m − $100m = $900m.
Step 3. If the distribution is **recallable**, LPs may have to return some or all of the $100m later, having paid interest on it in the meantime.

Takeaway: the DPI rose from 0.10x to 0.225x without a single asset being sold. The "distribution" is the LPs' own assets, returned to them as debt. This is the precise mechanism ILPA's guidance targets.

## Example 7 - Co-investment blended fee saving

An LP commits **$50m** to a fund (fees: 2% management, 20% carry) and is offered a **$50m** co-investment on a "no fee, no carry" basis.

Step 1. Blended management fee = ($50m × 2.0% + $50m × 0.0%) / ($100m total) = $1.0m / $100m = **1.0%**.
Step 2. The LP has doubled its exposure to the GP's deals (from $50m to $100m of capital deployed) while halving its average management-fee rate (from 2.0% to 1.0%).
Step 3. On carry, blended carried interest on gains is likewise roughly halved versus an all-fund program, because the co-invest portion bears no carry.

Takeaway: co-investing is the most direct lever an LP has to lower its all-in cost of PE exposure. The saving is real only if the underlying co-invest deal performs; remember the portfolio-company-level fees from Part 8.2.

## Example 8 - Deferred consideration lifts the buyer's IRR

A buyer agrees a $100m secondary price.
- **All cash at closing:** pays $100m on day 0. If the stake returns $140m in 4 years, IRR = (140/100)^(1/4) − 1 = (1.4)^(0.25) − 1 = **8.8%**.
- **50% deferred, interest-free, paid at end of year 2:** pays $50m on day 0 and $50m at year 2. Because half the capital is outstanding for less time, the same $140m return produces a higher IRR. Approximating the average capital outstanding, the money-weighted IRR rises to roughly **11% to 12%**.

Takeaway: deferral lets the buyer bid a higher headline price (good for the seller's optics) while still hitting its target IRR (good for the buyer). This is why roughly a quarter of LP deals and nearly a third of GP-led volume in 2025 used deferred structures (Jefferies).

## Example 9 - KS-PME (public market equivalent)

A fund has these cash flows, with the S&P 500 index level on each date in brackets.
- Year 0: contribution **$100** (index = 100)
- Year 2: distribution **$60** (index = 120)
- Year 4: distribution **$80** plus ending NAV **$0** (index = 150)

Step 1. Discount each cash flow by the index level relative to year 0 (divide by index, then the ratios are comparable).
   - Contribution PV factor: 100 / 100 = 1.000, so contribution term = $100 × 1.000 = $100.0.
   - Year-2 distribution: scaled by 100/120 = 0.8333, so $60 × 0.8333 = $50.0.
   - Year-4 distribution: scaled by 100/150 = 0.6667, so $80 × 0.6667 = $53.3.
Step 2. KS-PME = (sum of index-adjusted distributions and NAV) / (index-adjusted contributions) = ($50.0 + $53.3) / $100.0 = $103.3 / $100.0 = **1.03**.
Step 3. Interpret: a KS-PME of 1.03 means the fund returned about 3% more than the same cash flows invested in the S&P 500 would have. A value below 1.0 would mean the fund underperformed the index.

Takeaway: PME answers the only question that matters for an allocator who could have indexed: did the illiquid, fee-heavy fund actually beat the public market for the same timing of cash in and out? Over 25 years, US buyout PME has historically sat around 1.15 to 1.25, but recent-vintage PME has compressed toward roughly 1.05 to 1.12 as public markets ran and PE realizations stalled (Cambridge Associates benchmarks, as summarized in industry analysis; treat the exact recent figures as approximate).

# PART 11 - Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| LP (limited partner) | The investor that commits capital to a fund (pension, endowment, sovereign fund, insurer, fund of funds). Your team sits on this side. |
| GP (general partner) | The manager that raises the fund, makes investments, and earns fees and carry. Also called the sponsor. |
| Primary commitment | A commitment to a new, blind-pool fund, funded over time via capital calls. The "primaries" in your team's name. |
| Secondary | A purchase or sale of an existing fund interest or portfolio of interests, after the fund is partway through life. |
| LP-led secondary | A transaction initiated by an LP selling its fund interest(s); the GP is mostly a bystander who approves the transfer. |
| GP-led secondary | A transaction initiated by the GP, usually moving assets into a continuation vehicle; the GP is the driver. |
| Continuation vehicle / fund (CV) | A new fund run by the same GP that buys one or more assets out of an older fund to extend the hold and offer LPs a cash-or-roll choice. |
| Single-asset CV | A continuation vehicle holding one company, often a "trophy" asset. |
| Multi-asset CV | A continuation vehicle holding several companies. |
| Roll | An existing LP's election to stay invested by moving its interest into the CV rather than cashing out. |
| Status quo option | The ability for a rolling LP to keep the original fund's economic terms in the CV rather than accepting reset, usually higher, terms. ILPA-recommended. |
| Cash-out / sell election | An existing LP's choice to take cash now at the CV transaction price. |
| NAV (net asset value) | The reported fair value of a fund's holdings net of liabilities; the reference value for secondary pricing and for NAV loans. |
| Price as % of NAV | Secondary purchase price divided by reference NAV; the headline pricing metric. |
| Discount / premium to NAV | Pricing below par is a discount; above par is a premium. Discount = 1 − (price/NAV). |
| Deferred consideration | Paying part of a secondary price later, often interest-free, to improve the buyer's IRR and allow a higher headline price. |
| Preferred equity (secondary) | A financing giving the provider a priority claim on distributions up to a capped return, leaving upside with the holder. |
| Strip sale | Selling a pro-rata slice ("strip") across many positions rather than whole interests. |
| NAV-based facility / NAV loan | A loan to a fund secured by the value of its portfolio (NAV), used for portfolio support (~80%) or to fund distributions (~20%). |
| Subscription line (sub line) | Short-term fund borrowing secured by LPs' uncalled commitments, used early in fund life; can inflate reported IRR. |
| Recallable distribution | A distribution LPs may be required to return to the fund later. |
| Co-investment | A direct LP investment into a specific portfolio company alongside a GP's fund, usually on reduced or no fee and carry. |
| No fee, no carry | The common co-investment economic basis; does not preclude portfolio-company-level fees. |
| Warehousing / catch-up cost | Costs co-investors bear for the period the main fund held a deal before syndicating it to them. |
| DPI (distributions to paid-in) | Cumulative cash distributed divided by capital paid in; the realized-cash multiple LPs now prioritize. |
| RVPI (residual value to paid-in) | Residual NAV divided by paid-in capital; the unrealized portion. |
| TVPI (total value to paid-in) | DPI + RVPI; total realized plus unrealized value per dollar paid in. |
| MOIC (multiple on invested capital) | Total value divided by invested capital, usually gross and at the deal level. |
| IRR (internal rate of return) | The money-weighted, time-sensitive annualized return that sets NPV of all cash flows to zero. |
| PME (public market equivalent) | A method that benchmarks fund cash flows against a public index for the same timing; KS-PME > 1.0 means the fund beat the index. |
| J-curve | The early dip in a fund's net return and value, as fees and early markdowns hit before gains and distributions arrive; the curve later turns up. |
| Dry powder | Committed capital raised but not yet invested. |
| Vintage year | The year a fund begins investing; used to compare funds raised in similar environments. |
| Capital call / drawdown | The GP's request for committed capital from LPs as it is needed. |
| Committed vs paid-in capital | Committed is the total promised; paid-in (drawn) is what has actually been called. |
| Unfunded commitment | Committed capital not yet called; assumed by a secondary buyer. |
| Carried interest (carry) | The GP's profit share, typically about 20% of gains above a hurdle. |
| Hurdle / preferred return | The minimum return (often ~8%) LPs receive before the GP earns carry. |
| Management fee | The GP's annual fee, typically ~1.5% to 2% of commitments or invested capital. |
| GP commitment | The GP's own capital in the fund, a key alignment signal. |
| LPAC (LP advisory committee) | A committee of large LPs that reviews conflicts and approves certain GP actions, including NAV facilities and CV transactions. |
| Fairness opinion | An independent third-party opinion that a transaction price is fair, common in GP-led deals. |
| Fund of funds (FoF) | A vehicle that invests in multiple primary funds (and increasingly secondaries and co-invests) on behalf of its own investors. |
| Denominator effect | When public holdings fall, private allocations rise as a share of the total portfolio, pushing LPs over target and prompting sales or slower commitments. |
| Take-private (public-to-private) | A buyout of a publicly listed company, taking it private. |
| Sponsor-to-sponsor exit | A sale of a portfolio company from one PE firm to another. |
| Semi-liquid / evergreen fund | An open-ended vehicle offering periodic liquidity, central to the retail "democratization" of private markets. |
| SMA (separately managed account) | A custom, single-LP mandate; the fastest-growing "custom" capital category. |
| Tail-end fund | An old fund (often 10+ years) holding a few remaining assets; a common secondary sale candidate, priced at deep discounts. |

# PART 13 - Interview questions with model answers

**Q1. Walk me through how you would price an LP secondary stake.**
Start from the reference NAV and underwrite the remaining portfolio company by company, because a fund interest is a claim on those companies. Build a base-case projection of future distributions and an exit view, then discount to a target net IRR (typically mid-teens or higher for buyout). Convert that to a price as a percentage of NAV. Adjust for the unfunded commitment you must reserve to fund future calls, for NAV staleness since the reference date, and for cash flows between reference and closing. Consider structuring such as deferred consideration to improve your IRR and let you bid a higher headline price. In 2025 the average buyout stake cleared around 92% of NAV, so a bid near there would be market for a typical buyout portfolio; older or venture-heavy portfolios price lower.

**Q2. Why has DPI been so low, and why does the industry care more about it than IRR now?**
Three straight years of slow M&A and a closed IPO window meant GPs could not sell companies at attractive prices, so cash back to LPs collapsed. Bain shows distributions below 15% of NAV for four years running; McKinsey puts distributions at about 6% of PE AUM in the year to June 2025 versus a 16% historical norm. The industry shifted to DPI because IRR can be inflated by subscription lines and reflects unrealized marks, while DPI is the cash that actually funds pension and endowment obligations. An LP cannot spend an IRR.

**Q3. Why would a sophisticated LP ever sell a fund stake at a discount to NAV?**
Because it is buying certainty. A 90% bid looks like surrendering 10%, but the seller converts an uncertain five-to-seven-year stream into cash today, sheds the unfunded commitment and its drawdown risk, frees capital to redeploy or meet obligations, and exits the administrative drag of tail-end funds. For an LP over its target allocation (the denominator effect) or needing liquidity, that certainty is worth the discount. The sale is increasingly active portfolio management, not distress; about 40% of LP-led sellers in early 2025 were first-time sellers.

**Q4. Make the bear case on continuation vehicles. Then tell me how you would get comfortable.**
Bear case: the GP is on both sides of the trade, setting the price on an asset it sells to itself, with strong incentives to do the deal regardless of merit, namely a fresh fee stream, crystallized carry, and a bigger stake in a winner. Rolling LPs can be pushed into worse economics, and the structure can be a way to avoid an honest exit. To get comfortable I would require an independent fairness opinion and valuation shared before election, an LPAC conflict waiver, a status quo option with at least a 20-business-day election period, and, above all, evidence of alignment: the GP rolling its crystallized carry back into the CV. If the GP is reinvesting its own proceeds, the deal is probably about upside, not just fees.

**Q5. What is a NAV loan, and what is the controversy?**
A NAV loan is fund-level debt secured by the value of the fund's portfolio, sitting on top of company-level leverage. About 80% of usage supports portfolio companies and is uncontroversial. The fight is over the roughly 20% used to fund distributions, because a GP can borrow against the portfolio and send LPs cash that flatters DPI before a fundraise, without selling anything. The distribution is often recallable, so LPs can pay interest on cash later clawed back, and the leverage is frequently invisible to LPs whose LPAs are silent on the topic. ILPA's 2024 guidance does not ban them but demands LPAC consent for distribution use and standardized disclosure.

**Q6. How do co-investments lower an LP's cost of PE, and where is the catch?**
Co-investments are offered on reduced or no fee and carry, so blending them into a program cuts the all-in fee load and lifts net returns. Double your exposure to a GP at half the average fee and you have materially improved net economics, and co-invests have historically tended to beat their parent funds. The catch is twofold: "no fee, no carry" still permits portfolio-company-level transaction and monitoring fees, plus warehousing costs for late closers; and co-investing concentrates risk in single deals, raising adverse-selection risk that the GP is syndicating something it did not want to hold in full. The saving is only real if the deal is sound and you have the team to underwrite it fast.

**Q7. The fed funds rate fell from 5.50% to 3.75%. Why hasn't dealmaking fully normalized?**
Rates came down only partway and then stalled in 2026 because of a renewed inflation shock, so financing is cheaper than the peak but not cheap. Just as important, the structural overhang persists: roughly 32,000 unsold companies worth about $3.8 trillion, aging dry powder, and a bid-ask gap between sellers anchored to old marks and buyers pricing today's cost of capital. The 2025 rebound was real but narrow and megadeal-driven; the broad market is still working through the backlog, and cash returned to LPs remains depressed. Lower rates help at the margin but do not by themselves clear a multi-year logjam.

**Q8. What do you diligence when underwriting a primary commitment to a GP's next fund?**
Track record on a realized basis (DPI and PME, not just IRR and TVPI), and how much of the prior funds' value is still unrealized paper. Team stability and the depth of operational value-creation capability, because returns now come from operations rather than leverage and multiple expansion. Strategy consistency and discipline on entry multiples. Alignment via GP commitment and how carry is structured. Use of subscription lines, NAV facilities, and continuation vehicles, and how transparent the GP has been about them. Fund terms, including fees, hurdle, and the co-investment offered. And portfolio construction, pacing, and the realism of the fundraise target given a hard market.

**Q9. What is PME and why do LPs use it instead of just IRR?**
PME (public market equivalent) benchmarks a fund's actual cash flows against what the same cash flows would have earned in a public index, controlling for timing. The Kaplan-Schoar version is the ratio of index-discounted distributions plus NAV to index-discounted contributions; above 1.0 means the fund beat the index. LPs use it because the real alternative to an illiquid, fee-heavy fund is indexing, so the honest question is whether the fund beat the public market for the same timing of capital in and out. IRR alone cannot answer that. Historically US buyout PME has run around 1.15 to 1.25 over long horizons, though recent vintages have compressed as public markets ran and PE realizations stalled.

**Q10. LP-led volume and GP-led volume were roughly equal in 2025. What does each tell you about the market?**
LP-led volume (about $125bn, ~52%) reflects LPs manufacturing their own liquidity and rebalancing in a low-distribution world, increasingly as routine portfolio management rather than distress. GP-led volume (about $115bn, ~53% growth, 48% of the market) reflects GPs using continuation vehicles to hold winners and return cash to LPs without fire-selling. Together they show the secondary market has become the structural release valve for a stalled exit environment, and that both sides of the LP-GP relationship are using it deliberately.

**Q11. A GP shows you a 35% net IRR. What follow-up questions do you ask?**
What is the DPI, and how much of the value is realized cash versus unrealized marks? Is there a subscription line, and how long is it kept outstanding, since that can add 200 to 500 basis points of IRR without changing cash returned? What is the MOIC, which the line cannot flatter? What is the vintage, and how does the PME look against the relevant public index over the same period? A 35% IRR with a 0.3x DPI is a very different fund from an 11% IRR with a 1.9x DPI, and the second has returned far more cash.

**Q12. Why are the largest GPs pulling away from the rest of the industry?**
Scale compounds advantages: bigger funds can write the equity checks for the megadeals that drove 2025, can fund operational value-creation teams, can offer LPs the co-investment they now demand, and can consolidate smaller managers. In a hard fundraising market, capital concentrates with brands LPs trust, so funds under $500m shrank to about 13% of fundraising while GP consolidation roughly doubled. The flip side is that median and smaller managers face a tougher fundraise and weaker access, which widens performance dispersion and makes manager selection the whole game for an LP.

**Q13. What is the J-curve, and how do secondaries and co-investments interact with it?**
The J-curve is the early dip in a fund's net value and return, as management fees and conservative early marks hit before gains and distributions arrive, with the curve turning up later in life. Secondaries can mitigate it for a buyer, because purchasing a fund several years in means buying past the dip, into a portfolio that is already maturing and closer to distributing, often at a discount. Co-investments can also dampen the early drag because they typically carry little or no fee, so they do not deepen the fee-driven part of the curve.

**Q14. If you had to flag one risk the consensus is underpricing right now, what would it be?**
The quiet leverage building up off balance sheet. NAV loans and continuation vehicles both keep assets and debt inside the system rather than realizing them, and subscription lines flatter reported IRRs. Each is defensible individually, but together they let the industry report healthier-looking performance and liquidity than the underlying cash supports, while adding layers of fund-level leverage that many LPs cannot fully see. If exits stay slow and rates do not fall, the gap between reported marks and realizable value is where the next disappointment comes from. That is the bear case an LP team should keep underwriting against, not assume away.
