## What "primaries" means

A **primary commitment** is an LP investing in a fund at its formation — committing capital to be called over time as the GP deploys it into deals. It is distinct from buying an existing fund stake in the secondary market, or investing directly alongside a fund as a co-investor. The LP is contracting to supply capital to a manager for roughly a decade, with very limited ability to exit. Because private-market fund performance is highly dispersed — top-quartile buyout funds from 2015–2019 vintages sat around 2.3x–2.7x net TVPI versus bottom-quartile funds that lost money in real terms — manager selection is the central task of primary investing.

The uncomfortable framing: past fund performance is a **weak predictor** of future performance in buyout. Performance persistence that made manager selection look skillful in early studies has substantially eroded post-2000. Most of an LP's realized edge comes from **access** (getting into capacity-constrained funds already chosen), **terms** (paying less in fees), and **pacing/portfolio construction** (committing steadily across vintages). Diligence mostly screens out frauds and disasters rather than identifying alpha.

Yet you do it anyway because: (1) avoiding the bottom quartile is worth more than finding the top, given enormous dispersion; (2) the bottom tends to persist — poor managers stay poor or fail to raise again; and (3) bad terms compound forever.

## The four-pillar DD framework

LP primary diligence runs four workstreams in parallel. Each must clear independently; failing any one can kill the deal, and ODD and Legal teams often hold a formal **veto**.

| Pillar | Core question | Kill power |
|---|---|---|
| **Investment DD (IDD)** | Can this team generate above-public returns repeatably? | Recommends |
| **Operational DD (ODD)** | Can we trust this firm with our cash and reporting? | Veto |
| **Legal / Terms** | Are the LPA economics and governance acceptable? | Veto on terms |
| **ESG** | Are there material non-financial risks; does this meet our mandate? | Veto for mandate-bound LPs |

Roughly half of fund-manager failures trace to **operational** breakdowns rather than bad investing, which is why ODD has risen to near parity with IDD since the Madoff, Bayou, Amaranth, and Weavering scandals.

A typical institutional timeline: 3–6 months for a fund-of-funds; 6–18 months for a large pension or endowment building conviction, including monitoring the GP across prior funds before committing.

## The sourcing funnel: re-ups vs. new managers

A large LP may see hundreds of PPMs (Private Placement Memoranda) per year and formally diligence only a small fraction. The sourcing funnel has two distinct branches:

**Re-ups (existing manager relationships)**
- You already have prior-fund data, reference networks, and GP access.
- The question shifts: has the GP maintained the team, strategy, and discipline that earned the prior allocation? Has fund size crept up (strategy drift)? Has DPI come in on pace?
- Re-ups are faster to diligence but carry anchoring risk — prior commitment bias can cause analysts to miss deterioration.

**New manager sourcing**
- Referrals from co-investors, consultants, placement agents, other LPs, and conference networks.
- First-time funds (spin-outs, new platforms) require independent attribution of the team's track record from the prior firm, which is often disputed.
- Screening questions: Is the strategy differentiated? Is the edge articulable and testable? Does the team have relevant attribution? Are the terms LP-favorable to compensate for uncertainty?

A useful discipline: frame every potential investment as a hypothesis the GP wants you to accept, and your job is to find the version of reality in which the claim is false.

## The process: Screening → IC → Commitment → Monitoring

### Stage 1 — Initial screening
- Review the PPM and any track-record summary.
- Apply a quick filter: Is the strategy in mandate? Is the team cohesive with verifiable attribution? Are terms in range? Any ODD knock-outs (no admin, regulatory issues)?
- Output: Pass/fail for full diligence. Most LPs screen out the large majority at this stage.

### Stage 2 — Full diligence (IDD + ODD + Legal + ESG run in parallel)
- **IDD**: Rebuild track record from cash-flow-level data; assess team, strategy, edge, drift.
- **ODD**: Administrator, valuation governance, audit, compliance, cyber, controls.
- **Legal/Terms**: Full LPA redline against ILPA norms; side-letter negotiation.
- **ESG**: Policy, integration, portfolio KPIs, SFDR classification if relevant, DEI data.
- Reference calls (on-list and off-list), site visits, background checks on principals.
- Output: Draft IC memo with recommendation, key risks, and conditions.

### Stage 3 — IC deliberation and vote
- IC memo presented to the Investment Committee.
- The IC will stress-test the bear case, probe the team assessment, and evaluate terms.
- IC votes: approve, reject, or approve with conditions (e.g., require LPAC seat, independent administrator, fee concessions before close).

### Stage 4 — Commitment and close
- Side-letter negotiation (MFN elections, bespoke reporting, LPAC seat, fee terms).
- Legal execution of LPA subscription documents.
- Capital call schedule begins; subscription line of credit may delay the first calls.

### Stage 5 — Monitoring
- Ongoing after the commitment: quarterly reports, capital call and distribution tracking, annual meeting, reference-call updates.
- Monitor for strategy drift, key-person departures, ODD changes, and performance vs. vintage benchmark.
- Re-up decision informed by the full track record now available.

## The IC memo structure

The IC memo is simultaneously a decision document, a record of diligence performed, and the document that will be re-read if the fund disappoints. Write it to be falsifiable: state the thesis, then honestly state what would have to be true for it to fail.

**Standard IC memo outline:**

1. **Recommendation and summary** — Proposed commitment; fund name, target/hard cap, strategy, geography; one-paragraph thesis; top reasons to do it and top risks; conditions to commit.
2. **Firm and GP overview** — History, ownership, AUM, prior funds, regulatory standing.
3. **Team** — Key partners, individual deal attribution, key-person identification, turnover, succession, GP commitment (amount, how funded, distribution across partners).
4. **Strategy and edge** — The strategy in one sentence; the claimed edge and the evidence; drift check versus prior funds.
5. **Market opportunity** — The opportunity set, competitive dynamics, key macro risks.
6. **Track-record analysis** — Net IRR/TVPI/DPI/RVPI by fund and vintage vs. named benchmarks; gross-to-net spread; deal-by-deal distribution; top-deal concentration test; value-creation bridge; loss ratio; realized vs. unrealized; PME vs. the appropriate index.
7. **Terms summary and ILPA alignment** — Fee/step-down/offset, carry/hurdle/catch-up/waterfall, clawback quality, GP commitment, key-person/removal, recycling, sub-line, LPAC; each term flagged LP-favorable / market / GP-favorable.
8. **ODD summary** — Administrator, auditor, valuation governance, compliance, cyber, BCP, insurance, segregation, findings and remediations; ODD conclusion (pass/conditional/fail).
9. **ESG / responsible investment** — Policy, integration, reporting, SFDR, DEI.
10. **Portfolio fit and pacing** — Strategy/geography/vintage diversification; denominator-effect context.
11. **Risks and mitigants** — Each material risk with its mitigant and the residual risk accepted; the bear case.
12. **References and verification** — Named and blind calls, site visits, third-party reports.
13. **Recommendation, proposed size, and conditions** — Restated cleanly.
14. **Appendices** — Full track-record tables, cash-flow detail, fee model, LPA term sheet redline, DDQ.

## Underwriting expected returns: base / bull / bear scenarios

A disciplined LP builds a simple model of expected TVPI and net IRR across three scenarios rather than relying on the GP's single-point case.

**Framework:**

- Start from the fund's stated strategy (deal size, leverage, hold period, target sectors).
- Choose benchmark EBITDA-growth, multiple-expansion, and leverage-paydown assumptions for each scenario.
- Apply a gross-to-net haircut (typically 5–7 points of IRR; 0.4x–0.6x of MOIC) to convert gross to net.
- Stress the unrealized NAV by haircutting RVPI (commonly 10%–40% depending on mark vintage and asset type).

**Base case:** median-cohort assumptions for the strategy and vintage — roughly 1.6x–1.8x net TVPI and 12%–14% net IRR for buyout.

**Bull case:** top-quartile execution, favorable exit environment — roughly 2.3x–2.7x net TVPI and 18%–22% net IRR.

**Bear case:** the realistic downside — one or two deals impaired, exit market closed for 2–3 years, marks haircut 25%–30%. If the bear case still shows capital preservation and a PME above 0.8x, the downside is acceptable.

**Key sensitivities to test:**
- What happens if RVPI is marked down 30%? Does the fund still clear the hurdle?
- What happens if the top one or two deals are removed? (Concentration test.)
- What happens if hold periods extend by 2 years? (IRR sensitivity to timing.)
- What happens if exit multiples compress by 1–2 turns? (Multiple-expansion sensitivity.)

The subscript-line effect is also worth modeling: ask for returns with and without sub-line impact, because the line can inflate IRR by 3–5 percentage points without adding value.

## The current environment (2025–2026)

Global buyout deal value rebounded roughly 44% to about $904 billion in 2025 and exit value rose about 47% to roughly $717 billion, but distributions to LPs stayed stubbornly low and fundraising remained a grind (Bain Global Private Equity Report 2026). There is roughly $1.2 trillion of buyout dry powder, nearly a quarter of it more than four years old.

**DPI has overtaken IRR and TVPI as the metric LPs fixate on**, because GPs are sitting on large unrealized marks they cannot convert to cash, and 2018-vintage DPI of about 0.6x is running below the historical norm of about 0.8x at the same age. Re-up decisions now hinge on DPI.

Translation for the underwriting process: scrutinize realizations, treat NAV marks as a hypothesis, and assume the GP's pitch needs to be backed by realized cash.
