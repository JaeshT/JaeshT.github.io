## Deal flow and syndication

Co-investments do not arrive through a competitive market — they flow from the GP to its most trusted, most capable LPs. Understanding the sourcing and syndication process is essential for any LP operating in this space.

### How co-investments are originated

The GP originates all deal flow. It sources, negotiates, and controls the asset. Once the GP identifies that it needs additional equity — whether because the deal is larger than the fund's check, because a concentration limit has been reached, or because it wants committed equity before signing — it turns to its co-investment network.

That network is not random. GPs ration access to co-investment as a **relationship currency**, allocating it to:

- The largest, most strategic LPs in the current fund
- LPs who have demonstrated speed, discretion, and the ability to commit without excessive process friction
- LPs who are prospective commitments to the *next* fund
- Occasionally, third-party strategic co-investors (family offices, corporates, sovereign wealth funds) when they bring more than capital

**Practical implication:** a strong co-investment program is built on strong primary relationships. LPs without meaningful primary fund commitments are rarely given first-look co-investment access. The synergy between the primaries program and the co-investment program is structural, not coincidental.

### Syndication mechanics

Once the GP decides to syndicate, it typically:

1. Identifies the equity gap (total equity needed minus what the fund is putting in)
2. Allocates the gap among a short list of pre-approved co-investors
3. Sends a brief deal summary or teaser, often under NDA
4. Sets a deadline for expressions of interest — often measured in days, not weeks
5. Provides the full data room to those who respond positively
6. Collects soft commitments, then formal commitments on the GP's timeline

The LP has essentially no leverage on timing. The deal either happens on the GP's schedule or the LP is left out.

## Mid-life vs new co-underwrites

Not all co-investments are offered at deal signing. There are two structurally distinct timing points:

### New (at-acquisition) co-investments

The most common form. The GP is buying a company and invites the LP to participate at the same time and at the same entry price. The LP's capital is used to fund the initial acquisition. This is the clearest case of pari passu treatment and the strongest alignment signal.

### Mid-life (continuation and follow-on)

A less common but growing category. The GP already owns the company (it is in an existing fund) and is either:

- **Seeking a continuation vehicle**: the GP rolls the asset into a new SPV, typically because the original fund is nearing the end of its life or the GP wants to hold the company longer than the fund's term allows. Existing fund LPs may be offered the chance to roll their interest. New co-investors may be offered fresh capital alongside.
- **Seeking follow-on capital**: the portfolio company needs additional equity for a bolt-on acquisition, a rescue round, or a capital structure fix, and the GP is looking for outside capital alongside the fund's follow-on.

**Mid-life co-investments require additional caution:**

- You are coming in at an intermediate valuation, after some of the GP's value-creation thesis has played out — you need to underwrite whether the remaining upside justifies the current entry price.
- In a continuation vehicle, scrutinize why the GP wants to extend the hold: genuine long-term value thesis or asset-stranding to preserve management fees?
- Follow-on capital in a rescue scenario has very different risk characteristics from at-acquisition co-investment. Understand where you sit in the capital structure relative to existing equity and debt.

## Compressed timelines

Speed is the operational signature of co-investment. It is also where many LPs fail.

| Dimension | Primary fund DD | Co-investment DD |
|-----------|-----------------|------------------|
| Typical timeline | 4 to 12 weeks, with flexibility | 2 to 4 weeks, sometimes days, on the GP's clock |
| What is being judged | A manager and a blind pool | A specific, named asset |
| Who sets the clock | Largely the LP | The GP's deal process and the seller's timeline |
| Certainty | The fund will close | The deal may never happen (co-underwriting) |

Key points on timeline management:

- **Co-underwriting can require a commitment in three to four weeks, with no certainty the GP even wins the deal.** The LP is on the GP's timeline, which is on the seller's timeline. There is no extension to suit the LP's IC calendar.
- This forces process discipline: pre-cleared diligence frameworks, a standing IC that can convene quickly, template legal documents, and a clear and fast "no" process.
- **Speed is itself a selection pressure.** The best deals go to the LPs who can move; slow LPs are left with the leftovers, turning slowness into self-inflicted adverse selection.
- **Timeline pressure can be a tactic.** An unnaturally compressed timeline that makes genuine independent diligence impossible is a red flag. If the GP will not give you enough time to do the work, pass.

## Working from the GP's model and re-underwriting it

In co-investment, the LP does not build its own financial model from scratch. It receives the GP's model and must:

1. **Understand and stress the GP's own assumptions** — what drives the base case, which assumptions are most sensitive, and what the bear case looks like
2. **Re-underwrite the key inputs independently** — market growth rate, margin trajectory, leverage capacity, exit multiple
3. **Identify where the GP's view and your own diverge**, and whether those divergences are material to the decision
4. **Assess whether the GP's model reflects the GP's genuine conviction or is a marketing document**

This is fundamentally different from primary fund work, where you judge whether the GP's *process* is sound. In co-investment, you are also judging whether the GP's *analysis of this specific asset* is sound. That requires both trusting the GP and independently verifying the most important levers.

**LP skepticism in practice:** experienced co-investors focus most heavily on:

- **EBITDA add-backs and normalizations** — inflated EBITDA shrinks apparent leverage and flatters entry quality. Every add-back needs a reason.
- **Revenue assumptions** — is the base case within the range of what the market and the company's history support, or is it the optimistic scenario labeled "base"?
- **Exit multiple assumptions** — this is usually the swing variable in a co-investment return. Sensitize it. A thesis that requires the same or higher entry multiple at exit is fragile.
- **Leverage headroom** — what happens to covenants and interest coverage if EBITDA comes in 15% to 20% below the base case?

The GP has spent weeks or months on the asset; the LP has days to two weeks. The LP cannot replicate the GP's diligence in that time. The LP's job is to identify the two or three things that would make the deal fail and stress-test those specifically.

## The synergy with the primaries relationship

The primary fund commitment is the foundation of co-investment access. This synergy runs in both directions:

**Primaries → co-investment:**
- GPs share the best co-investment opportunities with LPs who have demonstrated commitment to the relationship through primary fund participation
- Primary fund DD builds familiarity with the GP's strategy, team, and deal style — which directly shortcuts co-investment DD on GP assessment
- Primary LPs receive information in the GP's annual meetings and quarterly reports that creates context for co-investment opportunities from the same GP

**Co-investment → primaries:**
- Co-investing deepens the LP's understanding of how a specific GP actually operates at the deal level, not just at the fund-strategy level
- Track record on co-investments from a GP informs whether to commit to the next primary fund
- Building a reputation as a reliable, decisive co-investor earns a seat at the table for future primary fundraising discussions

**The risk of over-dependency:** an LP that relies entirely on primary GPs for co-investment will have concentrated sourcing, limited negotiating power, and potentially high correlation between the primary portfolio and the co-investment portfolio. Sophisticated co-investment programs diversify co-investment sources across multiple GP relationships, sectors, and vintages even while maintaining deep primary commitments.

## Key process disciplines

An LP serious about co-investment must have:

1. **A pre-approved diligence framework** that can be deployed immediately on receipt of a deal, without needing to reinvent the process each time
2. **A standing IC or delegated authority** that can make a decision in days, not weeks — or a clear escalation path for fast approval
3. **Template legal documentation** for SPV participation that does not need to be negotiated from scratch each time
4. **A clear "fast no" culture** — the ability to decline quickly and with explanation, which preserves the GP relationship and does not waste either party's time
5. **A watch list** of GPs with whom the LP has strong primaries relationships and where co-investment access is being cultivated, so deal flow is not a surprise
6. **A portfolio-fit tracker** so any new co-investment can immediately be assessed against current sector, geography, vintage, and single-name exposures before a decision is made
