## What is a co-investment?

A **co-investment** is a direct minority equity investment by an LP into a single portfolio company, made **alongside** a GP's fund but **outside** the fund's main commingled structure. The GP leads the deal — sourcing, negotiating, structuring, controlling, and monitoring the company. The co-investing LP rides alongside in a passive, non-controlling position.

Key mechanics:

- **Deal-by-deal, not blind-pool.** A co-investment is a specific, named company you can underwrite today, not an unseen future portfolio.
- **Fee-light or fee-free.** Capital sits outside the fund, so the co-investor typically pays little or no management fee and little or no carried interest.
- **Structured through an SPV.** The GP creates a single-asset special purpose vehicle into which co-investors commit. Capital is called and deployed almost immediately, not over a multi-year drawdown.
- **Usually offered to existing LPs.** The co-investor is generally already an LP in the GP's fund, though GPs sometimes invite prospective LPs or third parties.

Think of a primary fund commitment as buying a diversified, professionally managed basket on a delayed-deployment schedule, and a co-investment as cherry-picking one holding from that basket, at a steep discount on fees, but bearing that single holding's idiosyncratic risk in full.

## Why LPs pursue co-investments

1. **Net-return enhancement through fee savings.** No-fee/no-carry economics preserve the gross return that fees and carry would otherwise skim. On standard "2 and 20" economics this is worth a substantial fraction of the gross multiple (see worked examples below).
2. **Lower blended cost of the whole program.** A primary commitment plus a no-fee co-invest sleeve produces a lower all-in fee load and a higher blended net return than the fund alone.
3. **Capital deployment at scale.** Large allocators struggle to deploy at scale; co-investment lets them put more dollars to work with a manager they already trust, faster than fund commitments alone permit.
4. **Selectivity and exposure control.** Co-investing lets an LP tilt toward sectors, geographies, or deal types it wants more of, and away from those it does not.
5. **Transparency.** Unlike a blind-pool commitment, the LP sees the actual company, model, and thesis before committing.
6. **J-curve mitigation.** Immediate deployment with no fee drag means capital starts working sooner and is not eroded by years of management fees, flattening and shortening the J-curve.
7. **Relationship deepening and capability building.** Co-investing strengthens the GP relationship and builds the LP's own direct-investing muscle.

Demand structurally exceeds supply. A StepStone survey of 145 GPs and 420 funds cited in Bain's 2025 Global Private Equity Report found co-investment volume up roughly 30% versus pre-pandemic, and **only about half of LPs with appetite were actually able to participate.** Co-investment capacity is rationed, which is precisely why GPs use it as a relationship currency.

## Why GPs offer co-investments

GPs do not give away economics out of generosity. They offer co-investment because it solves concrete problems:

1. **Check-size / capacity on large deals.** A single fund will not — and per its own concentration limits often *cannot* — write the full equity check for a large target. Rather than club with a rival sponsor or take on more leverage, the GP fills the equity gap with LP co-investment capital. This is the original and still dominant driver.
2. **Concentration discipline.** Most LPAs cap how much of a single fund can go into one deal (commonly 10% to 20% of commitments). Co-investment lets the GP do a large deal without breaching its own concentration limit.
3. **Fundraising leverage and LP relationship management.** Offering no-fee/no-carry co-investment is a powerful sweetener to reward the largest and most strategically important LPs and to win their commitments to the *next* fund.
4. **Speed and certainty of capital.** In a competitive auction, a sponsor that can show committed equity moves faster and bids more credibly. Reliable co-investors are a competitive weapon.
5. **Cheaper than the alternatives.** The GP avoids bringing in another control sponsor (and sharing governance) or over-equitizing the fund.

The uncomfortable corollary: **a GP that needs your capital on a deal has decided not to put more of its own fund's capital in.** That can be benign (concentration limits, fund nearly fully invested, deal larger than the fund's sweet spot) or a warning sign (limited GP conviction). Distinguishing the two is the core diligence task.

## The economics: no-fee/no-carry and the blended-return impact

### The fund baseline: "2 and 20"

A standard buyout fund charges roughly a **2% annual management fee** and **20% carried interest** on profits, the latter payable only after LPs receive their capital back plus a **preferred return (hurdle)**, commonly **8%**, often followed by a **GP catch-up**. Management fees frequently step down after the investment period (e.g., from committed capital to invested capital).

These two charges attack returns from different angles:

- **Management fee** is a drag on *capital*: the LP pays it whether or not the fund makes money, reducing the capital actually put to work.
- **Carried interest** is a drag on *profit*: it skims 20% (above the hurdle) of the upside.

### The co-investment difference

Co-investments are typically **no-fee/no-carry**, meaning the GP is generally not directly compensated on the co-invested portion of the deal. That means:

$$\text{Net co-investment return} \approx \text{Gross co-investment return}$$

That single identity is the whole product. On a deal where the fund LP nets 2.2x after carry, the co-investor on the same deal at the same entry keeps the full 2.5x. The 0.3x difference is pure fee saving, not skill.

### Fee-drag elimination and J-curve effect

Co-investments flatten and shorten the J-curve through two mechanisms:

1. **No fee drag.** With no annual management fee, there is no fee-driven erosion pulling early NAV below cost.
2. **Immediate deployment.** SPV capital is called and put to work essentially at once, rather than sitting as an uncalled commitment or being deployed slowly over a multi-year investment period.

**Illustrative J-curve (net TVPI by year, hypothetical):**

| Year | Fund (2 and 20) | No-fee co-invest |
|------|-----------------|------------------|
| 1    | 0.92x           | 1.00x            |
| 2    | 0.88x           | 1.05x            |
| 3    | 0.95x           | 1.20x            |
| 4    | 1.15x           | 1.45x            |
| 5    | 1.45x           | 1.80x            |

*These figures are illustrative, not measured. The shape is the point.*

**Caveat:** mitigating the J-curve is not reducing risk. Faster deployment means faster, concentrated single-asset exposure.

### Worked return figures

**Example 1 — Carried interest saved on a single deal**

Inputs: LP equity = $10.0M; gross exit multiple = 2.5x; hurdle = 8%; hold = 5 years; carry = 20%; 100% GP catch-up.

- Gross exit value: $2.5 \times \$10.0\text{M} = \$25.0\text{M}$
- Gross profit: $\$25.0\text{M} - \$10.0\text{M} = \$15.0\text{M}$
- Preferred return: $\$10.0\text{M} \times (1.08^5 - 1) = \$4.693\text{M}$
- GP catch-up: $\frac{0.20}{0.80} \times \$4.693\text{M} = \$1.173\text{M}$
- Remaining profit split 80/20: LP gets $\$7.307\text{M}$, GP gets $\$1.827\text{M}$
- **GP carry total** = $\$1.173\text{M} + \$1.827\text{M} = \$3.000\text{M}$ (= 20% × $15.0M)
- **Fund LP net MOIC: 2.20x** ($22.0M)
- **Co-investor net MOIC: 2.50x** ($25.0M)

Carry saved = $3.0M; MOIC uplift = **+0.30x**, about **13.6%** more profit, purely from fee economics.

**Example 2 — Fund-level gross-to-net wedge**

Inputs: Commitment = $100M; lifetime management fees = $15M; gross deal multiple = 2.5x; carry = 20%, hurdle cleared, full catch-up. Invested capital = $100M − $15M = **$85M**.

- Gross proceeds on invested capital: $2.5 \times \$85\text{M} = \$212.5\text{M}$
- Net profit before carry: $\$212.5\text{M} - \$100\text{M} = \$112.5\text{M}$
- Carry: $20\% \times \$112.5\text{M} = \$22.5\text{M}$
- **LP net: $190.0M → net MOIC 1.90x**

Gross **2.50x → net 1.90x**. The total wedge is **0.60x**, consuming roughly **24%** of the gross multiple. A no-fee/no-carry co-investment on the same underlying deal keeps the full 2.50x.

**Example 3 — Blended portfolio return**

Inputs: Fund commitment = $100M at net **1.90x**; co-investment sleeve = $25M at net **2.50x**.

- Fund value: $100\text{M} \times 1.90 = \$190\text{M}$
- Co-invest value: $25\text{M} \times 2.50 = \$62.5\text{M}$
- Total value: $\$190\text{M} + \$62.5\text{M} = \$252.5\text{M}$ on $125M deployed
- **Blended net MOIC: 2.02x**

Adding a 25% no-fee co-invest sleeve lifts the blended net MOIC from **1.90x to 2.02x** (+0.12x, about +6% relative), with the gain coming entirely from fee structure.

**Example 4 — The fee cushion and the adverse-selection break-even**

- Carry-only cushion: break-even co-invest gross = fund net = **2.20x**. Cushion = 2.50 − 2.20 = **0.30x = 12%** of gross MOIC.
- Fees-plus-carry cushion: break-even co-invest gross = **1.90x**. Cushion = 2.50 − 1.90 = **0.60x = 24%** of gross MOIC.

The fee saving buys a cushion of roughly **12% to 24% of gross MOIC**. A co-investment can be that much worse on a gross basis and still match or beat the fund net of fees.

### Caveats

- "No fee/no carry" is the norm, not a law. Some GPs charge reduced fees or carry on co-invest, or impose them on dedicated co-investment *funds*. Read the SPV terms.
- Co-invest is not cost-free to the LP: the LP bears its own underwriting, legal, and ongoing monitoring costs, plus a share of SPV/deal expenses. These are real and rise with deal complexity.

## The adverse-selection debate

### The theory

**Adverse selection** (the "lemons" problem): if the GP knows more about a deal than you do, and gets to choose which deals to share, then the deals offered may be systematically worse than the ones the GP keeps for the fund alone. The GP might share deals that are too large for conviction, too risky, or where it wants to lay off exposure. This is sometimes called a **winner's curse** for co-investors.

### The bear case: Fang, Ivashina and Lerner (2015)

Studied direct investments by **seven large institutions over 1991 to 2011**:

- **Co-investments underperformed the corresponding funds**, consistent with apparent adverse selection in the deals made available.
- **Solo** (no-GP-alongside) deals **outperformed**, suggesting an information advantage that co-investors lack.
- Co-investments were also **poorly timed**, concentrated in large deals near market peaks.

### The bull case: Braun, Jenkinson and Schemmerl (2020)

Used a **large deal-level sample from three fund-of-funds managers**, covering 464 funds, free of the FoF's own selection bias:

- **No evidence of adverse selection.** Gross-return distributions of co-investable deals and other deals in the same funds were statistically similar.
- Simulating net returns under a range of fee structures, **buyout co-investment net returns were economically and statistically higher than fund net returns**, and **reasonably sized diversified portfolios** significantly outperform the funds.
- Critical caveat: this is a **portfolio** result. One-off deals carry high dispersion; the outperformance comes from diversification plus the fee saving, not from individual deal magic.

### Practitioner data points

- **Cambridge Associates ("Making Waves", 2015)** found co-investments outperformed most in **low-volume vintage years** (early 2000s, 2009) when markets were volatile and entry multiples were low. *Note: this study compares co-investments **gross of fees** against sponsoring funds **net of fees**, which structurally tilts results in favor of co-investments — treat its magnitude with caution.*
- **StepStone data** found pre-signing co-investments averaged **2.7x gross TVPI** versus **2.2x for post-signing**, suggesting earlier-stage access performs better.
- **Adams Street (2025)** argues adverse selection is a "myth," reasoning that GPs depend on long-term LP relationships. Note this is an interested party (a co-investment manager).

### How to reconcile (the synthesis)

The papers reach opposite headline conclusions partly because they measure different things:

- FIL measures the **realized choices of specific institutions** (what deals they were offered, picked, and when). BJS measures the **full deal universe** of the funds, stripping out investor selection.
- FIL finds co-invests gross-underperform the fund's deals; BJS finds gross parity. Both agree the **fee saving helps net returns**.
- Where they converge: **timing and concentration dominate.** Co-investment supply peaks in frothy markets (large deals, high multiples), which is exactly when forward returns are worst.

### The portfolio and diversification caveat

The key formula for concentration risk:

$$\sigma_{\text{portfolio}} \approx \frac{\sigma_{\text{deal}}}{\sqrt{N}}$$

A single co-investment bears the full $\sigma_{\text{deal}}$. A program of 20 co-investments cuts dispersion to about **22%** (1/√20) of a single deal's. Moving from one deal to 20 also caps any single impairment at roughly 5% (1/20) of the program.

**The defensible LP position:** adverse selection is a *real risk to be managed*, not a reason to avoid co-investing. You manage it with deal-by-deal selectivity, scrutinizing GP alignment and motive for sharing, avoiding over-deployment in hot markets, and **diversifying** across enough deals that idiosyncratic and selection risk wash out. Cambridge explicitly advises against a "do every deal" approach for this reason.
