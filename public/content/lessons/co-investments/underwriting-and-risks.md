## Dual underwriting: the GP, the asset, and portfolio fit

Co-investment underwriting is fundamentally different from primary fund due diligence. In primary fund work, you are underwriting a manager and a strategy. In co-investment, you must underwrite **three things simultaneously**:

1. **The GP** — do you trust their judgment, execution capability, and alignment on this specific deal?
2. **The asset** — is this a good company at a fair price with a credible value-creation thesis?
3. **Portfolio fit** — how does this deal change your overall exposure, and does it fit?

All three must clear. A great company brought by a GP you do not trust or at a price that does not work is a pass. A reliable GP bringing a deal that doubles down on an exposure you already own too much of is also a pass. The interaction of all three is the job.

---

## The six underwriting lenses

### 1. Market

- **Size, growth, and structure:** TAM/SAM, growth rate, cyclicality, and fragmentation.
- **Competitive dynamics:** the company's defensible position — moat, switching costs, market share, pricing power.
- **Secular tailwinds vs headwinds:** regulation, technology disruption, demand durability.
- **Cycle positioning:** co-investment supply peaks in frothy markets; ask whether you are buying at a cycle high.

### 2. Company / business quality

- **Business model and unit economics:** how does it actually make money and is that durable?
- **Revenue quality:** recurring vs one-time, customer concentration, churn, contract length, backlog.
- **Margins and operating leverage:** cash conversion (does EBITDA become cash?).
- **Value-creation levers:** organic growth, M&A roll-up, margin expansion, multiple re-rating — which are realistic and which are heroic?

### 3. Management

- **Quality, tenure, and equity stake:** alignment with the value-creation plan.
- **Track record** executing the specific kind of plan being underwritten (growth, roll-up, turnaround, etc.).
- **GP's relationship with management** and any planned changes post-close.

### 4. Financials and the model

- **Historical financials:** growth trajectory, margin history, working-capital intensity, capex intensity, and cash conversion.
- **EBITDA add-backs and normalizations:** every add-back inflates apparent EBITDA and shrinks apparent leverage. Scrutinize each one. Aggressive add-backs are a red flag.
- **Base-case model:** are the revenue growth and margin assumptions within the range of historical performance and market benchmarks, or are they the optimistic scenario relabeled "base"?
- **Leverage analysis:** entry debt/EBITDA, structure (fixed vs floating rate), maturities, covenant headroom, interest coverage, and headroom under stress.
- **Value-creation bridge:** decompose the projected equity return into:

$$\text{Equity Return} = \text{EBITDA Growth} + \text{Margin Expansion} + \text{Multiple Change} + \text{Debt Paydown}$$

A thesis driven mainly by **multiple expansion** is fragile — it depends on the exit market cooperating. A thesis driven by **EBITDA growth and deleveraging** is sturdier because it is within the GP's operational control.

### 5. Deal structure

- **Entry valuation:** EV/EBITDA (and EV/Revenue where relevant) versus comparable transactions and trading comps. Is the price disciplined or full?
- **Pari passu treatment:** is the co-investor holding the same security, at the same entry price, with the same terms as the fund? Any structural subordination is disqualifying.
- **Sources and uses:** pro-forma cap table, co-invest slice, and how the equity is split.
- **Fee economics on the co-invest:** confirm no-fee/no-carry or the exact reduced terms, expense allocation between SPV and co-investors, and treatment of transaction fees.
- **Alignment:** GP's own dollars in the deal (fund commitment plus GP commitment), and management's rollover equity. If the GP and management are not meaningfully invested alongside you, ask why.

### 6. Downside / risk

This is where LP co-investors must be most disciplined, because there is no diversified pool to bail out a bad single-company call.

- **Downside case and break-even:** what set of assumptions returns 1.0x? How plausible is that scenario?
- **Loss scenario:** what does a recession, demand shock, margin compression, or refinancing failure do to the equity? Stress leverage explicitly — model the covenant breach scenario.
- **Exit sensitivity:** sensitize the exit multiple because it is usually the swing variable. What happens to IRR if the exit multiple is 1 to 2 turns lower than the base case?
- **Liquidity and exit routes:** realistic exit options (strategic buyer, sponsor-to-sponsor, IPO), hold period estimate, and whether any of these requires market conditions that do not currently exist.
- **Concentration fit:** how does this deal change the LP's overall exposure by sector, geography, vintage, and single name? Does it double down on something you already own through the fund?
- **The meta-question:** why is the GP sharing this deal, and does the answer survive scrutiny?

## Cash-flow resilience

Beyond point-in-time leverage metrics, underwrite the company's ability to service its debt through a cycle:

- **Interest coverage:** EBITDA / interest expense, and how this moves in a rising-rate environment if debt is floating rate
- **Free cash flow conversion:** does EBITDA translate to cash after capex and working capital? A business with high capex or working capital intensity burns cash even when profitable.
- **Covenant headroom:** typical maintenance covenants (net leverage, interest coverage) and how much EBITDA needs to fall before a breach. A 20% EBITDA cushion may sound comfortable, but in a cyclical downturn it can disappear quickly.
- **Maturity wall:** when does the debt mature? A company with leverage maturing in 2 to 3 years has refinancing risk that a longer-dated capital structure does not.
- **Refinancing risk in a stress:** if the base-case EBITDA does not materialize, can the company refinance at maturity, and at what cost?

## Leverage, covenants, and equity risk

Leverage is the single largest amplifier of both upside and downside in a co-investment. At typical buyout leverage of 4x to 6x EBITDA:

- A 20% EBITDA decline translates to a roughly proportional decline in equity value *before* accounting for covenant risk.
- A covenant breach can trigger a restructuring even before the company runs out of cash, particularly if the debt has maintenance covenants.
- **Floating-rate debt** in a rising-rate environment increases the sensitivity of cash flow to rate moves and shrinks the EBITDA-to-cash conversion.

**Rule of thumb:** stress the model to 80% of base-case EBITDA over the hold period and ask whether the company can service its debt, maintain covenants, and still produce a positive return. If the answer is no at 80%, the leverage is too high for the business's risk profile.

## Value-creation thesis vs multiple arbitrage

The distinction between **operational value creation** and **multiple expansion** is one of the most important in co-investment underwriting:

**Operational value creation** (sturdier thesis):
- EBITDA growth driven by revenue expansion and operational improvement
- Margin expansion through cost reduction, pricing power, or mix shift
- Deleveraging from free cash flow — equity value grows as debt is paid down
- All of these are within the GP's control and do not require market conditions to cooperate

**Multiple expansion** (fragile thesis):
- The thesis requires selling at a higher EV/EBITDA multiple than the entry price
- This depends on market conditions, appetite for the sector, and comparable transaction multiples at exit — none of which the GP controls
- In a co-investment context, you cannot afford for the return thesis to be primarily dependent on something the GP cannot control

**Interview point:** a well-constructed value-creation bridge should show the majority of projected equity return coming from EBITDA growth and deleveraging. If the IC memo shows two-thirds of the return coming from multiple expansion, that is a significant red flag. If the thesis requires holding multiples flat (entry = exit multiple), underwrite whether that is realistic given where we are in the cycle.

## Concentration and single-name risk

Unlike a primary fund holding 10 to 25 companies, a co-investment is one company. If it impairs, the LP takes the full loss on that capital, undiluted. This is the fundamental difference from fund investing.

The mathematics:

$$\sigma_{\text{portfolio}} \approx \frac{\sigma_{\text{deal}}}{\sqrt{N}}$$

**Implications for a co-investment program:**

- A single co-investment bears the full $\sigma_{\text{deal}}$
- 20 equal-weighted co-investments reduce dispersion to $1/\sqrt{20} \approx 22\%$ of a single deal's
- A single total loss in a 20-deal program costs 5% of the program; the same loss as a standalone co-invest costs 100% of that capital

**Practical concentration management:**
- Set per-deal sizing limits so no single impairment is fatal to the program
- Diversify across sectors, geographies, and vintages — deals in the same sector or vintage are positively correlated, which weakens the benefit of diversification
- Be especially cautious about deals that add to sector or GP concentrations already present in the primary fund portfolio
- Braun-Jenkinson-Schemmerl frame their outperformance result explicitly as a **diversified-portfolio** result — the fee-saving advantage only consistently materializes across a portfolio of deals, not in any single one

## When to pass even on a no-fee deal

The absence of fees does not make a co-investment a good investment. Pass when:

1. **The GP cannot give a clean, consistent reason for sharing the deal.** An unclear motive for sharing is a stronger signal than any financial metric.
2. **GP alignment is thin or terms are not pari passu.** Little fund or GP capital in the deal, or co-invest terms worse than the fund's, means the GP is not bearing the same risk.
3. **Co-invest economics appear "free" but are loaded with fees or expenses in the SPV**, or there are open-ended follow-on obligations.
4. **The value-creation thesis leans primarily on multiple expansion** or an aggressive exit multiple that requires market conditions to cooperate.
5. **Aggressive EBITDA add-backs** inflate entry quality and hide true leverage.
6. **Leverage will not survive a cyclical downturn** — tight covenants, floating-rate exposure, or near-term maturities in a stress scenario.
7. **The deal is outside the GP's strike zone** — new sector, new geography, or a deal size much larger than usual. A GP operating outside its competence zone is less likely to generate returns from operational improvement.
8. **The deal doubles down on an exposure the LP already has** through the fund, creating dangerous concentration without additional diversification benefit.
9. **The timeline makes genuine independent diligence impossible.** If you cannot do the work in the time available, pass. Speed pressure is sometimes a tactic, not just a constraint.
10. **Customer, supplier, or counterparty concentration** that is glossed over in the GP's materials.

**The meta-principle:** liking the company is necessary but not sufficient. The deal, the price, the alignment, and the portfolio fit all have to clear independently.

## Worked return figures

**Example 5 — Concentration and the power of diversification**

Inputs: per-deal return standard deviation $\sigma_{\text{deal}} = 1.00\text{x}$ (illustrative); deals assumed roughly independent and equal-weighted.

- **Single co-investment:** dispersion borne = full $\sigma_{\text{deal}} = 1.00\text{x}$. A total impairment costs 100% of that capital.
- **N = 20 deals:** $\sigma_{\text{portfolio}} \approx 1.00\text{x} / \sqrt{20} = 1.00\text{x} / 4.472 = \mathbf{0.224\text{x}}$

Moving from one deal to 20 cuts return dispersion to about **22%** of a single deal's, and caps any single impairment at roughly **5%** of the program. This is the mechanical reason Braun-Jenkinson-Schemmerl frame co-investment outperformance as a diversified-portfolio result.

**Example 4 — The adverse-selection break-even (fee cushion)**

The fee saving creates a cushion: a co-investment can underperform the fund's deals on a **gross** basis by up to the fee cushion and still match or beat the fund on a **net** basis.

- **Carry-only cushion:** gross 2.50x → fund LP net 2.20x → cushion = **0.30x = 12%** of gross MOIC
- **Fees-plus-carry cushion:** gross 2.50x → fund LP net 1.90x → cushion = **0.60x = 24%** of gross MOIC

Interview point: adverse selection has to be quite severe to overwhelm the fee advantage, which is why disciplined, diversified co-investing tends to win net even if individual deal selection is imperfect.

## Key formulas

**Value-creation bridge (equity return decomposition):**

$$\text{Gross Equity MOIC} = f(\text{EBITDA growth}, \text{margin expansion}, \text{exit multiple}, \text{debt paydown})$$

**Leverage stress (illustrative):**

$$\text{Interest Coverage} = \frac{\text{EBITDA}}{\text{Interest Expense}}$$

**Portfolio dispersion:**

$$\sigma_{\text{portfolio}} \approx \frac{\sigma_{\text{deal}}}{\sqrt{N}}$$

**Fee cushion / adverse-selection break-even:**

$$\text{Fee Cushion} = \text{Fund Gross MOIC} - \text{Fund Net MOIC}$$

$$\text{Break-even co-invest gross MOIC} = \text{Fund Net MOIC}$$
