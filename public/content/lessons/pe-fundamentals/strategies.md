## Overview

Private equity encompasses a wide range of investment strategies that differ on **what they buy, how much leverage they use, where returns come from, and the risk/return profile**. Understanding these differences is essential for an LP-side analyst evaluating manager mandates and building a diversified portfolio.

A useful framework for comparison:

| Strategy | Control? | Leverage | Return Source | Risk Level | Typical Hold |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Buyout (LBO) | Yes | High | Deleveraging + ops + multiple | Medium-High | 4–7 years |
| Growth Equity | No (minority) | Low/none | Revenue/earnings growth | Medium | 3–5 years |
| Venture Capital | No (minority) | None | Power-law winner outcomes | High | 7–12 years |
| Mezzanine/Private Credit | No | N/A (lender) | Interest + equity kicker | Low-Medium | 3–7 years |
| Distressed | Sometimes | Low | Restructuring / turnaround | Medium-High | 2–5 years |
| Secondaries | Indirect | Low | Discount to NAV + J-curve skip | Low-Medium | 3–7 years |
| Co-investment | Yes/No | Varies | Mirrors lead GP's deal | Medium-High | 4–7 years |
| Fund-of-Funds | Indirect | None | Diversification + selection | Low-Medium | 10–12 years |
| Infrastructure | Often | Moderate | Yield + inflation linkage | Low | 10–25 years |

## 1. Buyout (Leveraged Buyout / LBO)

**What it is:** Acquisition of a **controlling stake** in a mature, cash-generative company, financed with a significant layer of **debt** (the "leveraged" part) on top of equity.

**Return levers — the "three sources of PE returns":**
1. **Deleveraging:** Using the company's cash flow to pay down debt, so the equity slice grows as the debt shrinks
2. **Operational improvement:** Revenue growth, margin expansion, leadership changes, add-on acquisitions
3. **Multiple expansion:** Selling at a higher EV/EBITDA multiple than the purchase multiple

Entry multiples are high and rising: North American average buyout multiples reached **11.9× EBITDA** and Europe a record **12.1× EBITDA** in 2024, which is why operational value creation now matters more than financial engineering. In software buyouts, revenue growth drove ~52% of value creation and multiple expansion ~42%, with margin growth a small remainder.

**Profile:**
- Dominant strategy by capital raised
- Home of the mega-funds (Blackstone, KKR, Apollo, EQT, Advent, etc.)
- Buyouts represent ~72% of secondary-sale volume by strategy
- Moderate-to-high risk; leverage amplifies both gains and losses
- Target net IRR typically in the high teens to low twenties; target net multiple 1.8–2.5×

## 2. Growth Equity

**What it is:** **Minority** investments in companies that are already growing and often already profitable, with **little or no leverage**. Returns come almost entirely from revenue and earnings growth, not debt or financial engineering.

**Positioning:**
- Later and less risky than venture (companies are already past early-stage execution risk)
- Non-control, lower-leverage, and lower target multiples than buyout
- The company typically does not need restructuring — it needs capital to scale

**Profile:**
- Lower loss rates than venture
- No leverage means no LBO-style amplification, for better or worse
- Returns driven purely by growth; exit typically via IPO or strategic sale
- Hold periods typically 3–5 years
- Target net IRR mid-teens to low twenties

---

## 3. Venture Capital (VC)

**What it is:** **Minority equity** in **early-stage, high-growth** companies, with no leverage.

**Key characteristic — power-law returns:**
Returns follow a **power law**: most investments fail or return little, and a tiny number of huge winners (10×, 100×, or more) drive the entire fund's return. The manager's task is selecting for the right tail of outcomes, not avoiding losses across the portfolio.

**Profile:**
- Highest dispersion of all strategies
- Longest path to liquidity (many investments take 7–12 years to exit)
- Early-stage VC often pre-revenue or pre-profit
- Categories: seed, Series A/B/C/D, growth stage, late-stage
- Industry concentration common (tech, biotech, fintech)
- Target net returns vary enormously by fund quality; top-quartile VC can produce 3×+ net; bottom-quartile often returns less than invested

## 4. Mezzanine / Private Credit

**What it is:** Providing **junior debt or hybrid instruments** (mezzanine debt, unitranche loans, preferred equity) to companies, usually alongside a buyout transaction or to support growth. Not pure equity — the manager is primarily a lender.

**Return sources:**
- **Cash interest** on the loan (floating or fixed rate)
- **PIK (Payment-in-Kind) interest** that accrues and compounds
- **Equity kicker** (warrants or equity co-investment) that provides upside if the company performs well

**Sub-types in the broader "private credit" universe:**
- **Direct lending:** Senior secured loans to mid-market companies (the largest and fastest-growing sub-sector)
- **Mezzanine:** Junior subordinated debt with equity-linked upside
- **Distressed debt:** Buying below par (covered separately below)
- **CLOs/structured credit:** More complex structured vehicles

**Profile:**
- Lower risk than equity strategies (contractual cash flows, senior/secured in direct lending)
- Return primarily income-oriented; less J-curve than equity funds
- Shorter hold (3–7 years) and more predictable distributions
- Has become a major asset class: private credit AUM now exceeds $1.5 trillion globally

---

## 5. Distressed / Special Situations

**What it is:** Investing in the **debt or equity of troubled or financially stressed companies**, sometimes to control them through a restructuring ("distressed-for-control"), sometimes to trade the securities.

**Includes:**
- Distressed debt trading (buying below par in anticipation of recovery or control)
- Turnarounds (operational restructuring of struggling businesses)
- Rescue financing (providing capital to prevent bankruptcy)
- Special situations (event-driven: spin-offs, litigation, regulatory change)

**Key characteristic — counter-cyclical:**
The best opportunities appear in **downturns and credit stress** when prices fall and motivated sellers are forced to transact. Distressed managers often raise "opportunity funds" to deploy in downturns.

**Profile:**
- Skill set is legal, financial, and operational restructuring rather than growth
- Returns highly vintage-dependent (best vintages are post-crisis)
- Moderate-to-high risk; loss rates can be significant if restructuring fails
- Hold period depends on the restructuring: typically 2–5 years

## 6. Infrastructure

**What it is:** **Long-duration real assets** with stable, often contracted or regulated cash flows: toll roads, airports, utilities, pipelines, renewables, and increasingly digital infrastructure (data centers, fiber networks, cell towers).

**Key characteristics:**
- Lower target returns than buyout but **lower risk**: cash flows are often contractually locked in or regulated
- Very **long hold periods** (10–25 years)
- Steady **cash yield** (regular distributions from operating cash flow)
- Frequently **inflation-linked revenues** (infrastructure assets often pass through inflation)
- The 2025 mega-exit of Aligned Data Centers at $40 billion shows how digital and traditional infrastructure have converged

**Profile:**
- Often classified separately from PE but managed by the same mega-managers
- Good diversifier in an LP portfolio: low correlation to public equities
- Target net returns typically in the high single digits to low-to-mid teens

## 7. Secondaries

**What it is:** Buying or restructuring **existing** fund interests rather than committing to new funds. Two main sub-types:

### LP-Led Secondaries
An LP **sells its existing fund stakes** (its commitments plus already-funded NAV) to a secondary buyer, usually at a discount or premium to NAV, to obtain liquidity or rebalance. Buyout LP stakes traded at roughly **94% of NAV** in H1 2025, up from below 90% in 2022 as discounts narrowed.

### GP-Led / Continuation Vehicles (CVs)
The **GP moves one or more assets** from an aging fund into a new vehicle, giving existing LPs the choice to cash out or roll over while new secondary investors provide fresh capital and the GP keeps managing the asset.

Continuation vehicles let GPs hold winners longer and manufacture liquidity when exits are scarce. They also embed a **structural conflict of interest** — the GP sits on both sides of the trade — which is why ILPA added specific guidance and why pricing/process scrutiny is intense.

**Why LPs use secondaries:**
- Shorter or absent **J-curve** (buying assets already part-way to maturity)
- Faster **DPI** (distributions arrive sooner)
- **Diversification** across vintages and managers
- **Visibility** into the underlying portfolio (no blind pool)
- Potential to buy at a **discount to NAV**

**Market size:** The secondaries market reached $226–240 billion in 2025 (a record), roughly doubling from ~$114 billion in 2023.

## 8. Co-Investment

**What it is:** Investing **directly alongside a GP** in a single deal, typically with **no management fee and no carry** ("no fee, no carry"), on top of an LP's fund commitment.

**Why GPs offer co-invest:**
- A deal is too large for the fund alone
- Deepens the LP relationship
- Provides extra equity for the transaction

**For the LP:**
- Lowers **blended cost** of exposure (no fee/carry on co-invest dollars)
- Increases exposure to a deal the GP already chose
- Usually done on the GP's timeline (quick underwriting required)

**Trade-offs:**
- **Concentration:** exposed to one company, not a diversified pool
- **Adverse selection risk:** is the GP sharing its best deals or only the ones it cannot fully fund?
- **Speed:** limited time for independent due diligence

---

## 9. Fund-of-Funds (FoF)

**What it is:** A vehicle that **invests across many underlying PE funds** on behalf of its investors, providing diversification and outsourced manager selection.

**Advantages:**
- Diversification across GPs, strategies, and vintages
- Access to hard-to-reach or over-subscribed managers
- Outsourced selection for smaller LPs without in-house expertise

**Decisive disadvantage — the second layer:**
A FoF adds a **second layer of fees and carry** stacked on top of the underlying funds' fees. For example, if a FoF charges 1% and 10% on top of an underlying fund's 2% and 20%, the total fee drag compounds significantly. This is hard to justify unless the FoF's manager-selection skill is exceptional or it provides access the LP truly cannot get directly.

Larger LPs build **in-house primaries teams** precisely to capture PE exposure without the FoF fee layer. "Direct fund investing" in the LP context means investing straight into funds (not through a FoF).

## Risk, Return, and Hold: A Deeper Look

### Return Distribution Comparison

| Strategy | Expected Net IRR Range | Net Multiple Range | Key Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buyout | 15–22% | 1.8–2.5× | Leverage + entry multiple |
| Growth Equity | 15–20% | 2–3× | Growth execution risk |
| Venture | Wide (0–50%+) | 0.5–5×+ | Power-law; most fail |
| Private Credit | 8–14% | 1.2–1.5× | Default / credit risk |
| Distressed | 15–25% | 1.5–2.5× | Restructuring risk |
| Infrastructure | 8–14% | 1.5–2.0× | Regulatory / political |
| Secondaries | 12–18% | 1.5–2.2× | NAV mark accuracy |

*Note: All ranges are illustrative targets; actual outcomes vary widely by vintage, manager quality, and market conditions.*

### The Manager-Selection Imperative

A critical insight for LP-side analysts: across all strategies, the **dispersion between top-quartile and bottom-quartile managers is enormous** — often the difference between a 1.5× and a 3×+ net multiple. The asset class's average net-of-fee return is contested and often modest. The entire economic case for investing in PE rests on **manager selection and access**, not the asset-class beta.

Top-quartile buyout funds have shown KS-PME values well above 1.2 (beating a public index by 20%+) while bottom-quartile funds fell below 0.7 (losing to the index by 30%). Manager selection is the job.
